Chapter 1: The Human

Understanding the strengths, cognitive limits, and sensory systems of the human operator in Human-Computer Interaction.

1.1 Why the "Human" Comes First in HCI

Before we can design interactive systems, we must understand the capabilities and limitations of the humans who will use them. An interface designed without respect to human psychology and physiology is guaranteed to fail, regardless of how fast or feature-rich the underlying software is.

Core HCI Principle
"Fast algorithm ≠ usable software. Feature-rich app ≠ user-friendly app. Systems must be designed for real, imperfect humans, not idealized users."
  • Computers are built for humans: The human is the center of the system. If the user cannot navigate or understand the software, the system as a whole fails.
  • Humans are imperfect: We forget passwords, make mistakes under stress, have limited attention spans, and get distracted easily.
  • Real-world examples:
    • Google Search autocomplete: Mitigates human spelling mistakes and reduces typing effort.
    • Netflix recommendations: Reduces cognitive overload by suggesting content based on past behavior.
    • ATM confirmations: Prevents catastrophic errors (like leaving the card behind) by returning the card before dispensing money.

1.2 The Model Human Processor (MHP)

Developed by Card, Moran, and Newell in 1983, the Model Human Processor (MHP) is a simplified conceptual model that views human cognition as an information processing system similar to a computer. It helps designers predict how long tasks will take and where users will struggle.

2. Cognitive System: Process center (thinks, reasons, retrieves memory, decides).

1.3 Senses & Visual Limits

While humans possess five senses, HCI focuses primarily on Vision, Hearing, and Touch. Vision is the primary source of information, making its physiological limits central to screen layout and typography design.

Two Stages of Visual Perception
Stage 1: Physical Reception: Light enters the eye, passes through the lens, and is focused as a two-dimensional image on the photoreceptors of the retina.
Stage 2: Interpretation: The brain processes the raw retinal image, resolving ambiguities by drawing upon expectations, context, and mental frameworks. Meaning is constructed, not copied.
  • Skeleton Screens: When an app (like Facebook) loads, it shows gray placeholders. The human brain interprets this structure, filling the gaps to perceive that content is loading, which makes the app feel faster than it is.
  • Visual Angle & Size: Visual angle indicates how much of the field of view an object occupies. It depends on both the physical size of the object and its distance from the eye.
    Visual Angle (minutes of arc) = (3438 * Object Size) / Distance
  • Visual Acuity: The ability to perceive fine detail is limited. Normal vision can detect a single line at 0.5 seconds of arc and gaps between lines at 30–60 seconds of arc. If an icon or font is too small (visual angle is too narrow), it cannot be perceived.
  • Brightness & Luminance: Brightness is a subjective reaction to light levels, affected by object luminance. It is measured by the just noticeable difference. Visual acuity increases as luminance increases, but so does screen flicker.
  • Color Perception: Handled by cones on the retina, color is composed of three elements: Hue (wavelength), Intensity (brightness), and Saturation (color purity).
    ⚠️ Crucial limit: Blue acuity is the lowest. Small blue text or icons on dark backgrounds are incredibly difficult for the human eye to resolve.
  • Visual Illusions: Illustrate that interpretation is a constructed process. In the Ponzo illusion, context cues make us perceive lines of identical length as different sizes because the brain uses perspective rules.

1.4 Reading, Hearing, and Touch

HCI must account for the specific ways humans read text, process sounds, and register physical feedback.

Saccades & Fixations
Saccades: Rapid, jerky movements of the eye from one point to another (approx. 4-5 times per second). No information is processed during a saccade.
Fixations: Pauses between saccades where the eye rests on a word or target. Actual visual perception and comprehension occur only during fixations.
  • Reading Process: The brain perceives a visual word pattern, decodes it using an internal language model, and interprets it using syntax (structure), semantics (meaning), and pragmatics (contextual use). Scanning Stack Overflow or skim-reading documentation leverages saccadic jumps to find keywords.
  • Hearing (Auditory Channel): Provides environmental cues (distance, direction).
    • Outer Ear: Protects the inner ear structures and amplifies incoming sound waves.
    • Middle Ear: Transmits sound waves as physical vibrations across tiny bones to the inner ear.
    • Inner Ear: Converts vibrations into chemical and electrical impulses sent to the auditory nerve.
    • Sound Properties: Pitch (frequency), Loudness (amplitude), and Timbre (the quality or complexity of the sound, which allows us to distinguish a piano from a violin).
  • Touch (Haptic Channel): Crucial for visually impaired users and haptic feedback.
    • Receptors in the skin include thermoreceptors (heat/cold), nociceptors (pain), and mechanoreceptors (pressure).
    • Fingers are highly sensitive compared to the back or head due to dense mechanoreceptor distribution.
    • Kinesthesis: The awareness of body position and joint angles, which directly impacts keyboard comfort and mouse control.

George Miller's Chunking Rule
Short-term memory has a strict capacity limit of 7 ± 2 items, established by George Miller. To circumvent this, humans use chunking—grouping random characters or items into meaningful units.
Example: Remembering 265397620853 is extremely difficult, whereas chunked numbers like 44 113 245 8920 (or phone numbers grouped as 3-3-4) are easily held in working memory.
Baddeley's Working Memory Model
Working memory is not just a passive buffer, but active. It consists of:
• Central Executive: The manager. Controls attention and decides what helpers to send data to.
• Articulatory Loop: Verbal helper. Repeats words and numbers in your head (e.g., repeating an OTP).
• Visuo-Spatial Scratch Pad: Visual helper. Manages layouts, images, and positioning.
• Auditory Imaging System: Sound helper. Stores ringtones and pitch patterns.
  • Recency Effect: In free recall, humans remember the last few items presented much better than those in the middle. In menu design, place critical exit or entry links at the very top or very bottom to leverage this effect.
  • Information Entry into LTM: Occurs via rehearsal. Repetition combined with meaningful use creates stronger synaptic connections, which is why actions in daily-use apps (like Instagram swiping) become automatic.
  • LTM Categories:
    • Episodic Memory: Sequenced record of personal events and contextual experiences.
    • Semantic Memory: Structured network of facts, concepts, and rules.

1.6 Semantic Networks & Scripts

Semantic memory does not store concepts randomly; it organizes them in structured, graph-like mental schemas.

Semantic Networks
A Semantic Network represents knowledge as a graph:
• Nodes: Represent concepts or objects (e.g., Animal, Dog, Collie, Lassie).
• Links: Represent explicit relationships between concepts:
    - IS-A: Class-subclass relationship (Collie IS-A Dog; Dog IS-A Animal).
    - HAS-A: Attribute relationship (Dog HAS-A tail).
    - INSTANCE-OF: Specific example (Lassie INSTANCE-OF Collie).
  • Property Inheritance: Child nodes automatically inherit properties from parent nodes (e.g., Animal moves and breathes ➔ Dog inherits moves and breathes). This represents a highly efficient cognitive storage mechanism that allows humans to perform rapid inference on unseen systems.
  • Scripts: Schema representations of stereotypical knowledge about common, recurring situations.
    Example: "John took his dog to the surgery. After seeing the vet, he left."
    The reader automatically infers the dog was sick, the vet examined it, treatment occurred, and John paid. We do this because we have a pre-stored "visit the vet" script containing:
    • Entry conditions: Dog is ill, vet is open, owner has money.
    • Props: Examination table, medicine, surgical instruments.
    • Roles: Vet examines, owner pays, receptionist greets.
    • Scenes: Arriving at reception, waiting in room, exam, paying.
    • Results: Dog is better, owner is poorer, vet is richer.

1.7 Thinking, Reasoning, and Problem Solving

Thinking involves applying reasoning to solve problems and make decisions. HCI designers must map interaction sequences to match these reasoning channels.

Three Types of Reasoning
1. Deductive Reasoning: Derives logically necessary conclusions from given premises.
Example: "If it is Monday, John goes to work. It is Monday. Therefore, John goes to work." (Logically certain, but premise must be true).
2. Inductive Reasoning: Draws general rules from repeated observations.
Example: "Every elephant I have ever seen has a trunk. Therefore, all elephants have trunks." (Generalizes, not logically guaranteed).
3. Abductive Reasoning: Reasons backward from an observed effect to a plausible cause.
Example: "We know Sam drives fast when she has been drinking. We observe Sam driving fast. We infer Sam has been drinking." (Unreliable, as an emergency could also explain the speed).
  • Wason's Card Selection Task: Demonstrates human bias in inductive reasoning. Given cards showing A, B, Green, and Red, and the rule: "Any card with B on one side has Red on the other."
    To test this, most people select B and Red (confirmation bias). However, to truly test the rule, one must select B (to see if it has red) and Green (to ensure it does *not* have B on the back). This proves humans struggle with falsification logic in software.
  • Problem Solving Theories:
    • Gestalt Theory: Solving problems via sudden insight and restructuring the mental representation of a system.
    • Problem Space Theory (Newell & Simon): Mentally moving from an initial state to a goal state using operators (actions) and heuristics (rules of thumb) to navigate state options.
    • Analogy: Solving new problems by mapping the solution structure of a familiar domain to the new, unfamiliar domain.

1.8 Skill Acquisition, Errors, and Emotion

As users interact with a system over time, their behavior shifts from slow, conscious planning to fast, automatic execution. Designers must accommodate both novice and expert cognitive states.

Anderson's ACT Model of Skill Acquisition
1. Declarative Stage: The user learns explicit facts and rules about the system. Processing is slow, requiring heavy working memory load (e.g., following a recipe step-by-step).
2. Procedural Stage: Practice converts general rules into specific, compiled action sequences, freeing up working memory.
3. Generalization / Expert Stage: The user extracts common patterns, enabling automatic, rapid execution without conscious attention.
  • Skill-Based Errors: Paradoxically, as behavior becomes automatic, skilled users are prone to specific errors. If the context of a skilled task changes slightly, the highly familiar, automatic action sequence may override the user's conscious intention.
    Example: A driver intends to stop at a shop on the way home from work but drives straight past because the automatic "drive home" script overrides the weak intention.
  • Mental Model Mismatch (Lift Case): Errors also occur when a user's internal model of how a system works does not match the actual system logic. In a lift, clicking the floor button repeatedly doesn't make it move faster, but the user's mental model suggests "more input = faster response."
  • Emotion & Affect: Emotions directly alter cognitive styles:
    • James-Lange Theory: Physiological reactions occur first, and emotions are interpreted post-hoc ("We are afraid because we tremble").
    • Cannon's View: Body responses are too slow and non-specific to define distinct emotions.
    • Schachter-Singer Theory (Most useful for HCI): Emotion is a combination of physiological arousal and cognitive situational appraisal. A racing heartbeat is interpreted as *excitement* in a game, but *panic* if an exam submission portal crashes.
  • Individual Differences: Users are not uniform. Designers must account for long-term differences (gender, physical abilities, sensory disabilities), short-term variations (fatigue, stress, cognitive distractions), and changes over time (age).

Q1: A designer places small dark-blue labels on a black app interface to display system logs. Explain why this violates human visual perception constraints.

Answer: This design violates human visual limits because the cones in the retina are least sensitive to the blue wavelength, resulting in the lowest visual acuity for blue objects. Small font size reduces the visual angle, and placing low-intensity blue text on a black background yields poor contrast. The brain cannot resolve the details or fill the gaps under Stage 2 (Interpretation), causing severe eye strain.

Answer: In the Declarative stage, users rely on explicit rules and slow, conscious search in working memory to execute actions. In the Procedural stage, practice compiles these rules into automatic physical operations. Interfaces must support both: clear labels and wizard steps for declarative users (novices), and keyboard shortcuts or swipe gestures for proceduralized users (experts) to bypass the slow cognitive processor loop.

Chapter 2: The Computer

Examining the hardware, interaction devices, text entry methods, 3D systems, and mathematical models of computer input and output.

2.1 Interactivity & Text Entry Devices

Computers have evolved from slow, batch-processing machines into highly interactive devices. This interactivity is powered by a variety of input channels that translate human physical actions into digital data.

Text Entry Devices
• QWERTY Keyboard: The primary text entry device. Designed for mechanical typewriters to slow typists down and prevent key arms from jamming. Despite better alternatives, QWERTY persists due to social and technological inertia (high retraining cost, user resistance).
• DVORAK Keyboard: An optimized layout that groups common keys together, minimizing finger travel and increasing typing speed.
• Chord Keyboard: Uses very few keys (4–5 instead of 26+). Characters are entered by pressing key combinations (chords). Highly portable and fast to use once learned (e.g., Microwriter).
  • Numeric Keypads Layout Mismatch: Calculators use a layout with '123' on the bottom (primarily for entering numbers for calculation), whereas telephones and ATMs use a layout with '123' at the top. Designers must carefully choose the appropriate layout based on user context. A mismatch (e.g., trying to input an ATM PIN on a calculator layout) leads to errors due to motor memory confusion.
  • Phone pad and T9 entry:
    • Multi-press: Using numeric keys with multiple presses (e.g., pressing '4' twice and '3' twice to type 'he'). hello = 4433555[pause]555666.
    • T9 Predictive Entry: Pressing a single key per letter; a built-in dictionary guesses the correct word (e.g., hello = 43556). Auto-complete on WhatsApp is a modern descendant.
  • Handwriting Recognition: Translates pen-strokes on a digitizing tablet into text. Technical challenges include: capturing stroke path and pressure, segmenting joined-up writing, and coping with highly variable individual handwriting styles.
  • Speech Recognition: A natural form of interaction achieving high accuracy (~97%), but typically restricted to limited command vocabularies. Challenges include: natural language ambiguity, background noise, and accents or vocal differences.

2.3 Displays & Visual Output Systems

Visual output must match the human eye's refresh windows, lighting constraints, and location contexts.

Display Technologies
• Liquid Crystal Displays (LCDs): Utilize voltage applied to liquid crystals to change polarization and color. Because light is reflected (or back-lit) rather than emitted raw, it results in less eye strain than older CRT monitors.
• Cathode Ray Tube (CRT): Stream of electrons focused by magnetic fields onto a phosphor screen, causing it to glow.
• Situated Displays: Displays mounted in specific public places relevant to the location (e.g., Hermes displays beside office doors showing handwritten notes left via a stylus). Location dictates the meaning of the information.
  • Random Scan vs. DVST:
    • Random Scan (Directed-beam/Vector): Draws lines directly on the screen. Lines must be constantly redrawn (refreshed) to avoid fading, causing flicker if there are too many lines.
    • Direct View Storage Tube (DVST): Similar to random scan, but the image is written to a storage grid behind the phosphor, making it persistent. This yields no flicker, but the screen cannot be selectively erased; it must be completely cleared.
  • Special Displays (Non-screen Output):
    • LEDs: Simple status indicators (e.g., charging red/green lights). HCI benefit: gives instant feedback requiring zero cognitive effort, even when the screen is off.
    • Dials and Gauges: Display analog values instead of raw numbers. HCI reasoning: Humans spot extremes and rate-of-change much faster on an analog dial than reading numerical digits (e.g., easily seeing "too high" at a glance on a car speedometer).
    • Head-Up Display (HUD): Projects flight or speed data directly into the user's line of sight (e.g., fighter jets, helicopters). HCI reasoning: Prevents the danger of looking down at dashboard instruments in high-speed, safety-critical tasks.
  • Sound & Touch Outputs:
    • Sound Output: Audio signals confirm actions (e.g., chime on successful upload) or warn the user, reducing screen clutter.
    • Haptic Output: Vibration and force-feedback. Used in simulators (feel of surgical instruments) and automotive controls (e.g., BMW iDrive knob uses haptic feedback to let the driver feel "bumps" on selection menu items).

2.4 Physical Controls, Fluidity, and Constraints

Designing physical controls requires matching form to function and balancing real-world constraints.

Design Constraints
• Ergonomic: Buttons must be big enough and spaced appropriately to prevent accidental double-clicks.
• Physical: Size limitations of the hardware itself (e.g., high-voltage switches cannot be tiny).
• Legal & Safety: Placing hazardous controls (like stove dials) out of child reach or away from high-heat zones.
• Context & Environment: Microwave controls must use a smooth, wipeable membrane surface for easy cleaning in oily kitchens.
• Aesthetic & Economic: Must look visually appealing and remain affordable.
  • Fluidity & Natural Mapping: Fluidity is how naturally a physical movement matches a logical function.
    • Natural mapping: Turning a dial right increases volume; turning left decreases it. Joystick forward moves a game character forward.
    • Bad mapping: Volume up = rotate left, volume down = rotate right. This violates natural expectations, leading to immediate user frustration, cognitive load, and errors.
  • Smartphone Design Trade-Offs: Modern phones present an ergonomic compromise. To achieve a thin aesthetic (Aesthetic constraint), designers removed physical headphone jacks (space trade-off) and shrunk side buttons, which reduces tactile feedback (ergonomic trade-off).

2.5 Shannon Entropy in Interface Design

In HCI, Shannon Entropy measures the uncertainty or unpredictability of user choices on a screen. High uncertainty translates to a slower decision-making process for the user.

Mathematical Formula
H = - ∑ p(x) log2 p(x)

• H: Entropy (amount of uncertainty measured in bits).
• p(x): Probability of selecting a specific choice.
• log2: Base-2 logarithm (calculates uncertainty in bits).
  • Predictability and Click Rate:
    • Equal Probabilities = Maximum Entropy: If an ATM displays 4 options and the user is equally likely to click any ($p = 0.25$), entropy is $H = 2$ bits. This represents high uncertainty and slower selection times.
    • Unequal Probabilities = Lower Entropy: If one button is highly predictable (e.g. a "Login" button with $p = 0.85$ and others have $p = 0.05$), the entropy drops to $H \approx 0.85$ bits. The user makes the decision and clicks much faster.
  • HCI Design Applications:
    • Menu Design: Grouping 40 random items into categorised groups (like Burgers, Pizzas, Drinks) and highlighting bestsellers reduces uncertainty (lowers entropy), speeding up selections.
    • Predictive Text: Keyboards suggest the most likely word (e.g. "morning" after typing "Good mor..."). This reduces option entropy, accelerating user typing.

Exam Step-by-Step Entropy Calculation Tip

Problem: Calculate the entropy of a UI with probabilities $A=0.70, B=0.10, C=0.10, D=0.10$ (using $\log_2(0.70) \approx -0.515$ and $\log_2(0.10) \approx -3.322$).
Step 1: Apply formula $H = -[ p(A)\log_2 p(A) + p(B)\log_2 p(B) + p(C)\log_2 p(C) + p(D)\log_2 p(D) ]$
Step 2: Term 1: $0.70 \times (-0.515) = -0.3605$
Step 3: Remaining 3 identical terms: $3 \times (0.10 \times -3.322) = -0.9966$
Step 4: Sum terms: $-0.3605 + (-0.9966) = -1.3571$
Step 5: Negate sum: $H = 1.3571 \approx \mathbf{1.36\text{ bits}}$.
Since $1.36 < 2.00$, this design has lower uncertainty and will have faster response times.

Q1: A user experience team is deciding between two layouts for an interactive system. Layout X has 4 choices with selection probabilities $A=0.85, B=0.05, C=0.05, D=0.05$. Layout Y has probabilities $A=0.40, B=0.30, C=0.20, D=0.10$. Calculate the entropy of Layout X (given $\log_2(0.85) \approx -0.234, \log_2(0.05) \approx -4.322$) and compare it with Layout Y ($H_Y = 1.85$ bits) to decide which layout yields faster selection times.

Answer:
For Layout X: $H_X = -[ 0.85 \times \log_2(0.85) + 3 \times (0.05 \times \log_2(0.05)) ]$
$H_X = -[ 0.85 \times (-0.234) + 3 \times (0.05 \times -4.322) ]$
$H_X = -[ -0.1989 + 3 \times (-0.2161) ] = -[ -0.1989 - 0.6483 ] = 0.8472 \approx \mathbf{0.85\text{ bits}}$.
Comparing Layout X ($0.85$ bits) with Layout Y ($1.85$ bits), Layout X has significantly lower entropy. This indicates much lower uncertainty for the user (since option A is highly dominant). According to information theory in HCI, lower entropy translates to lower cognitive choice complexity and faster selection times. Therefore, Layout X is preferred for speed.

Chapter 3: The Interaction

Frameworks, ergonomics, execution loops, direct manipulation, and the communication channels between humans and computer systems.

3.1 User-Computer Communication & Terms

Interaction is a dialogue between the user and the computer system. The quality of this dialogue determines the overall usability of the product.

Interaction Levels
• Low Interaction (Batch Input): The user provides all input data at once, and the computer performs the task without further user involvement. There is no immediate feedback. E.g., submitting an assignment portal that shows no status update.
• High Interaction (Interactive Systems): The user gives instructions continuously, and the system provides instant, dynamic feedback. E.g., dragging files on a desktop, zooming photos with a pinch gesture.
  • Domain: The specific area of work under study (e.g., graphic design, accounting, academic enrollment).
  • Goal: What the user wants to achieve (e.g., "create a solid red triangle" or "book a flight to Karachi").
  • Task: The sequence of operations or actions required to achieve the goal (e.g., selecting the fill tool, clicking over the triangle borders, selecting red, and confirming).
  • Goal vs. Task Distinction: Goals represent the desired *end state*, while tasks represent the *means* (logical actions) used to get there. Designers must focus on matching the user's tasks to their goals without introducing unnecessary steps.

3.2 Norman's Execution-Evaluation Loop

Donald Norman's model of interaction describes the user-computer dialogue as a continuous execution and evaluation cycle consisting of seven distinct stages.

7 Stages of Norman's Loop
1. Establishing the Goal: User decides what they want to achieve (e.g., get more light).
2. Forming the Intention: Deciding how to achieve the goal (e.g., switch on the desk lamp).
3. Specifying the Action Sequence: Planning the exact movements at the interface (e.g., reach for lamp, press button).
4. Executing the Action: Carrying out the physical actions (e.g., pressing the switch).
5. Perceiving the System State: Observing the result shown by the system (e.g., seeing light emit).
6. Interpreting the System State: Understanding what the perceived state means (e.g., the bulb is working).
7. Evaluating the System State: Comparing the outcome with the original goal (e.g., checking if the room is bright enough).
Real-World Email Example
1. Goal: Send a query to the course instructor.
2. Intention: Use the university Webmail client to compose and send the message.
3. Action Sequence: Open browser ➔ click Webmail bookmark ➔ click "Compose" ➔ input address ➔ type query ➔ click "Send".
4. Execution: Click "Send" with the mouse cursor.
5. Perception: A notification popup appears reading "Email Sent successfully".
6. Interpretation: The message has entered the mail server queue.
7. Evaluation: The query is sent. Goal achieved.
  • Goal ≠ Intention ≠ Action: A single goal can have multiple intentions and actions. For the goal "get more light," the intention could be to switch on a desk lamp, flip a wall switch, open the curtains, or ask someone else. Each has different physical action sequences.
  • The Gulf of Execution: The gap between what the user wants to do and what actions the system allows. It asks: "How do I do what I want to do?"
    • Friction: Occurs when the switch is hidden, oddly shaped, or unlabeled, and the user doesn't know which button to press.
    • Solution: Make controls visible, standard, and clearly labeled.
  • The Gulf of Evaluation: The gap between what the system shows and what the user expects or understands. It asks: "Did it work? What happened?"
    • Friction: Occurs when a user clicks "Submit," the page stays blank, and there is no loading indicator or error state. The user wonders if the system crashed or registered the click.
    • Solution: Provide immediate visual/auditory feedback showing the system state clearly.

3.3 The Abowd and Beale Framework

Abowd and Beale extended Norman's loop by explicitly dividing the interactive system into four components, each using a distinct language. Translation between these languages is where usability issues occur.

4 Components & Languages
1. User (Task Language): Expresses goals, desires, and psychological intents (e.g., "I want to send a text").
2. Input (Input Language): Captures physical actions at the interface (e.g., keypresses, mouse clicks).
3. System (Core Language): The internal computational state, variables, and data structures (e.g., setting light_state = ON).
4. Output (Output Language): The visual, auditory, or haptic presentation of the system state (e.g., screen refresh, beep sound).
  • The Translation Cycle:
    • User ➔ Input (Articulation): The user translates their cognitive intent (Task Language) into physical actions at the input device (Input Language).
    • Input ➔ System (Performance): The input device translates physical events (Input Language) into state modifications inside the computer (Core Language).
    • System ➔ Output (Presentation): The computer translates core states (Core Language) into user-perceivable signals (Output Language).
    • Output ➔ User (Observation): The user perceives the output signals (Output Language) and translates them back into their cognitive task space (Task Language) to evaluate the result.

3.4 Ergonomics (Human Factors)

Ergonomics is the study of the physical characteristics of interaction between users, their tools, and the environment. It focuses on maximizing safety, comfort, and performance while minimizing error and fatigue.

Ergonomic Focus Areas
1. Arrangement of Controls & Displays: Controls must be grouped logically and placed within easy physical reach (e.g. placing emergency stop buttons in prominent zones).
2. Physical Environment: Lighting levels, background noise, temperature, and workspace posture (seating height, desk angle). Bad lighting causes glare and eye strain.
3. Health Issues: Preventing Repetitive Strain Injuries (RSI), posture-related back pain, and cognitive fatigue caused by long sessions.
4. Use of Color: Ensuring high contrast for readability and avoiding relying solely on color to convey critical safety states (accommodating color blindness).
  • Glass Interfaces vs. Dials & Knobs:
    • Traditional Controls (Dials/Knobs): Provide high tactile affordance, are continuously visible, are directly wired to equipment, and give strong spatial context cues. E.g., a physical dial on a washing machine.
    • Glass Interfaces (Screens): Are highly flexible and can change dynamically, but lack physical tactile feedback, require visual focus to locate buttons, and increase the risk of accidental activation.

3.5 Direct & Indirect Manipulation

Designers choose between direct and indirect manipulation styles based on the complexity, safety level, and distance of the task.

Direct Manipulation (Ben Shneiderman 1982)
• Visibility: The objects of interest are continuously represented on the screen.
• Rapid, Incremental, Reversible Actions: User actions (clicking, dragging, resizing) show immediate visual feedback and can be easily undone (Ctrl + Z).
• Syntactic Correctness: Hard to make syntax errors because the interface constraints prevent illegal actions (e.g. dragging a file directly into a folder replaces the terminal command mv file.txt folder/).
  • Indirect Manipulation (Mediated Control): The user does not interact with the real system directly. The interface acts as a mediator (agent) between the operator and the "plant" (real-world machinery or remote process).
    Example (SCADA systems for power grids): The operator adjusts voltage levels via sliders on a computer screen in a control room. Commands travel through networks to remote substations, and feedback comes later from sensors.
  • SCADA Issues: Sensor data may be delayed or sampled, meaning a breaker may show "Open" on the screen before the physical mechanism actually moves. A network lag can create a dangerous mismatch between the screen state and the physical reality.

3.6 Common Interaction Styles

HCI categorizes user-system dialog structures into nine classic interaction styles, each matching different user experience levels.

Style Description Key Pros Key Cons & Limits Examples
Command Line (CLI) Users type text commands with strict syntax. Direct access, very powerful and flexible, fast for experts. No visual cues, high cognitive memory recall load, steep learning curve. Git terminal (git push), PowerShell.
Menus Select from visible options on the screen. Rely on recognition over recall, easy for novices, structured. Slow for large option lists, screen space consumer. Settings menus, food delivery category lists.
Natural Language Interact using everyday speech or text. Extremely intuitive, no training required. High language ambiguity, context matching failures. ChatGPT prompts, Siri, Google Assistant.
Question/Answer System asks questions, user inputs simple choices. Guides users step-by-step, simple structure. Rigid, not suitable for complex multitasking. Setup wizards, installation dialog boxes.
Form-Fills Screen layout mimics paper data sheets. Easy to learn, supports editing/corrections. Messy fields cause high abandonment rates. Online registration portals, checkout forms.
Spreadsheets Grid of cells containing formulas and values. Automatic updates, supports complex cell calculations. Requires specialized training for formula syntax. Google Sheets, Microsoft Excel.
WIMP Windows, Icons, Menus, Pointers. Supports multitasking and window context switching. Can clutter screen, requires pointing accuracy. Windows OS, macOS Finder.
Point-and-Click Points and clicks/taps directly on visual targets. Extremely simple, fast, maps well to touchscreens. Limited to visible links, poor for heavy text entry. Hypertext Web browsing, kiosk maps.
3D Interfaces Spatial interaction in virtual/augmented worlds. High immersion, natural physical mapping. Causes fatigue (gorilla arm), motion sickness. Meta Quest 3 games, Apple Vision Pro spaces.
  • WIMP Interface Elements:
    • Windows: Independent work areas. Can be overlapping (one hides another), tiled (side-by-side), or cascaded (stacked with offset). Includes title bars and scrollbars.
    • Icons: Small pictures representing folders, files, or actions, supporting recognition over recall. Shrunk windows can be iconified to save space.
    • Menus: Present lists of actions. Items highlight when hovered. Includes menu bars, pull-downs, pop-ups (context menus), and cascading submenus. Design rule: Group by function, place dangerous actions (like delete) away from common ones, and apply Fitts' Law.
    • Pointers: Enable pointing and dragging. Cursor shape changes to show system modes (arrow = normal, crosshair = drawing, spinner = busy).
    • Buttons: Isolated regions to invoke actions (radio buttons are mutually exclusive, check boxes are non-exclusive).
    • Dialogue boxes: Information windows that pop up to inform of an event or request information (e.g. Save File dialog), disappearing once the task is complete.

3.7 Interaction Context & Virtual Crackers Case Study

Interaction is situated; it does not happen in isolation. It is affected by the physical, social, and organizational context, as well as the user's emotional engagement.

Experience Deconstruction
Rather than copying physical objects exactly, modern interaction designers perform Experience Deconstruction: breaking down what makes the physical interaction fun, social, or suspenseful, and translating those qualities into digital equivalents.
HCI Case Study: Virtual Christmas Crackers
A physical Christmas cracker is a cheap cylinder pulled by two people. It makes a loud bang, reveals a paper hat, a joke, and a tiny toy. It is a shared, co-located physical experience.
When translating this to a web application, designers did *not* just draw a picture of a cracker. They deconstructed the experience:
• Suspense: Recreated by showing a closed cracker page. The recipient must click "Open", triggering a slow animation that builds tension.
• Surprise: The loud bang is replaced by a sound file (WAV) playing.
• Shared Anticipation (Co-experience): The system tracks the recipient's progress in real-time and updates the sender's screen: Sent ➔ Opened ➔ Cracker pulled ➔ Viewed content. This creates a shared digital moment (similar to WhatsApp read receipts).
• Dressing up & Play: Recreated via links to print a paper mask or play a web game.

Possible Exam Questions with Solutions

Q1: A user clicks a button labeled "Update Records" in a database client. The screen remains unchanged for 8 seconds, and then suddenly flashes "Database Updated". Analyze this interaction sequence using Norman's Execution-Evaluation Loop and explain where the gulfs occur.

Answer:
This interaction sequence suffers from a massive Gulf of Evaluation. When the user executes the action (Stage 4) by clicking the button, the system enters a processing state. However, because the screen remains unchanged for 8 seconds, the user cannot perceive any state change (Stage 5 - Perception is blocked). The user cannot interpret the system state (Stage 6) and is left wondering: "Did the click register? Did the system crash?" This cognitive mismatch creates frustration because the user cannot evaluate if they are getting closer to their goal (Stage 7). The Gulf of Evaluation could be bridged by adding a loading spinner or progress bar showing the database transition state immediately upon execution.

Q2: Explain the differences between the Model-View-Controller (MVC) input-output routing model and the Presentation-Abstraction-Control (PAC) model, highlighting the differences in input handling.

Answer:
In MVC, the Controller handles input events, updates the Model (application state), and the View displays the state. A major issue is that input (like a mouse click) only has meaning in relation to what is currently shown on the screen (the View). Thus, the Controller must query the View, breaking absolute separation. In PAC, input and output are combined directly in the Presentation layer of each individual agent. The agent's Control component coordinates between its Presentation (UI) and Abstraction (logic) components. Multiple PAC agents communicate via their Control blocks in a hierarchical structure, making it cleaner for complex multi-view architectures.

Chapter 4: Paradigms

Tracing the chronological evolution and paradigm shifts of interactive computing, from batch systems to hypermedia and agency.

4.1 Introduction & Historical Shifts

A paradigm in HCI is a widely accepted example or model that shapes future designs. Paradigm shifts represent points in history where a new technology or creative concept redefined how humans and computers communicate.

Paradigm Concept
"Interaction paradigms arise from a combination of technological breakthroughs and creative design exploration. They are often recognized and codified *after* they succeed. A single modern device (like a smartphone) is multi-paradigmatic, combining GUIs, touch, voice, AI, and cloud paradigms simultaneously."
  • Batch Processing (Initial Paradigm): Impersonal computing where users had to write code, convert it to punch cards, submit them to a computer operator, and wait hours or days for printed paper output. There was no real-time interaction or feedback. If a single syntax error occurred, the user had to wait another 24 hours to find out.
  • Time-Sharing (The Birth of Interactive Computing): A technological breakthrough where a single mainframe switched rapidly between multiple users connected via terminals. This gave each user the illusion of dedicated access, introducing real-time feedback, interactive programming, and a continuous human-computer dialogue.

4.2 Chronological Shifts (VDUs to Personal Computing)

The transition from text-only terminal interfaces to visual, personal computing was driven by key pioneers in the 1960s and 1970s.

Pioneers of Visual Interaction
• Video Display Units (VDUs): In the 1950s-60s, military radar systems like SAGE (Semi-Automatic Ground Environment) introduced dynamic visual screens to support rapid decision-making.
• Ivan Sutherland's Sketchpad (1962): The ultimate visual breakthrough. Sutherland built a system allowing users to draw directly on a screen, manipulate visual objects, and store graphics in memory. Crucially: changing the drawing on the screen modified the computer's memory states directly.
• Douglas Engelbart's NLS / Mother of All Demos (1968): Engelbart aimed to "augment human intellect." He built the oN-Line System (NLS), demonstrating the mouse, hypertext links, real-time word processing, collaborative screens, and video conferencing. More importantly, he introduced the concept of **programming toolkits**: reusable component libraries that help developers build other systems.
  • Personal Computing (1970s Shift): Moving from centralized computers (time-sharing for programmers) to dedicated machines usable by non-experts.
    • Seymour Papert's LOGO: Designed programming to be child-friendly. Children typed simple natural commands (e.g. "go forward", "turn left") to control a physical or digital "turtle" that drew shapes. It proved that complex computing becomes more powerful when it is visual and playful.
    • Xerox PARC & Alan Kay's Dynabook: Kay envisioned a personal, visual, portable computer for children. At Xerox PARC, his team developed Smalltalk, which introduced object-oriented programming, visual programming environments, and window-based interfaces.
    • Xerox Star (1981): The first commercial WIMP system, introducing windows, icons, menus, and pointers to the general public. It matched human multitasking cognition by allowing users to switch tasks by clicking between window contexts.

4.3 Metaphors & Direct Manipulation

To help non-experts adopt personal computers, designers turned to visual metaphors and direct manipulation styles.

Metaphors in HCI
A Metaphor maps a new digital concept to something the user already understands from the physical world, reducing fear and accelerating the user's mental model formation (e.g., "Trash" icon for deleting files, "Folders" for directory structures, "Desktop" for the workspace).
  • When Metaphors Fail (The Typewriter Problem): Early word processors used the typewriter metaphor. On a physical typewriter, pressing the spacebar simply moves the carriage—space is not a character. In a digital word processor, space is a character that occupies memory and behaves like a letter. Professional typists got confused because the digital behavior violated their physical metaphor. Similarly, using the "floppy disk" icon for saving files persists today, even though most Gen Z users have never seen a physical floppy disk.
  • Language vs. Action Paradigm:
    • Language Paradigm: The user interacts by giving instructions to a mediator agent (e.g., typing rm file.pdf in a terminal).
    • Action Paradigm (Direct Manipulation): The interface *is* the world. The user acts directly on the objects (e.g., dragging file.pdf to the trash can icon).

4.4 Hypertext, Multi-Modality, and Ubiquitous Computing

Modern paradigms extend interaction beyond single screens and explicit, linear commands.

  • Hypertext & Hypermedia (Memex Vision): In 1945, Vannevar Bush proposed the Memex: a desk-like machine that stored massive amounts of microfilmed books and records, allowing users to create non-linear, associative links between them. It mirrored the human brain's natural associative thinking.
    • Hypertext: Non-linear text nodes connected via links.
    • Hypermedia: Extends links to cover text, images, audio, and video (e.g., YouTube video linking to a creator profile, which links to a community comment, which plays a audio snippet).
  • Multi-Modality: Using multiple human communication channels simultaneously to interact with a system (visual, audio, haptic).
    • Genuinely Multi-Modal: Simultaneous integration of channels (e.g., dragging a file with a gesture while saying "delete permanently").
    • Semi Multi-Modal (Most current systems): Users use channels sequentially or in isolation (e.g. either typing OR using voice input on a phone).
  • Computer-Supported Cooperative Work (CSCW): Focuses on groupware and collaboration through computers (e.g., multiple people editing a single Google Doc simultaneously). Interaction shifts from "one user, one machine" to "many users communicating via a shared digital space."
  • Agent-Based Interfaces: Software agents act on behalf of the user, filtering information, monitoring events, and learning behavior (e.g., Gmail spam filters, YouTube recommendation feeds). The user delegates control to the agent, representing a shift from direct manipulation.
  • Ubiquitous Computing (Mark Weiser): Weiser coined the idea of "computing everywhere." Computers are embedded invisibly into the environment. "The most profound technologies are those that disappear. They weave themselves into the fabric of everyday life until they are indistinguishable from it."
  • Implicit vs. Explicit Interaction:
    • Explicit: User gives clear commands (e.g. typing a search query).
    • Implicit / Context-Aware: The system senses user presence or context automatically and acts without explicit input (e.g., hallway motion sensors turning lights on when you walk in).

Possible Exam Questions with Solutions

Q1: Why was Ivan Sutherland's Sketchpad (1962) considered a revolutionary paradigm shift in HCI, rather than just an engineering improvement?

Answer: Before Sketchpad, computing was batch-processed or text-only, treating computers as calculators. Sutherland's Sketchpad introduced the concept of **visual interaction** where the screen was not just a display for outputs, but a dynamic input canvas. By allowing users to draw and manipulate objects on the screen using a light pen, it mapped visual actions directly to internal memory states. It showed that the user can construct and edit visual representations in real-time, laying the foundation for graphical user interfaces (GUIs), direct manipulation, and visual feedback.

Q2: Discuss the typewriter metaphor failure and explain the lessons it holds for modern designers when applying metaphors to new technologies.

Answer: The typewriter metaphor was applied to early word processors to make them familiar. However, the metaphor failed because of a fundamental logical mismatch: on a physical typewriter, pressing the spacebar is a mechanical movement that does *not* write a character, whereas in a digital system, a space is a character that occupies memory. This confused typists who tried to format text using spaces, leading to document layout breakages when editing. The lesson is that while metaphors reduce initial anxiety and help build an initial mental model, designers must ensure that the digital logic does not conflict with physical expectations, and must transition users toward the system's true digital capabilities once they are familiar.

Chapter 5: Interaction Design Basics

Applying goals and constraints, creating user personas, organizing site navigation, and setting layout structures for interfaces.

5.1 What is Design & The 4-Phase Design Cycle

Design is a structured problem-solving process that translates user goals into functional systems, operated within defined boundaries.

Interaction Design Definition
"Design is the process of achieving goals within constraints. Designers must balance user desires against technological limits, physical materials, time, and budget."
  • Goals: What the user wants to achieve (e.g., watch movies on a phone during commute, share screen capture).
  • Constraints: Set boundaries for the design:
    • Computer Limits: Latency, bandwidth, screen space.
    • Human Limits: Attention span, memory capacity, stress reactions.
    • External Limits: Budgets, deadlines, environmental safety guidelines.
  • Trade-Offs: Balancing competing constraints. E.g., making a phone thinner reduces battery capacity; adding a cooling system for mobile gaming requires a thicker body. A perfect system is badly designed because it represents excessive, unviable design effort.
  • The 4-Phase Design Process: A non-linear loop:
    1. Requirements: Identify *what is wanted* and what problems need solving (interviews, ethnography).
    2. Analysis: Organize raw requirements data into structural formats (task analysis, user scenarios).
    3. Design: Decide *how* it should look and behave (layout, navigation, dialogue notations).
    4. Implementation & Prototyping: Code and deploy the system, testing and iteratively refining the UI.

5.2 User Focus, Personas, and Scenarios

Good design requires a deep focus on real users and stakeholders, avoiding the temptation to design for a "generic user" or for the designer themselves.

Stakeholders vs. Users
• Direct Users: The primary operators clicking the screen (e.g. warehouse manager).
• Stakeholders: Anyone affected by the input or output of the system (e.g. clients, delivery drivers, salespeople, managers).
  • The Self-Reporting Trap (Say vs. Do): What users *say* they do rarely matches what they *actually* do.
    • Example: A student reports checking notifications 10 times a day, but logs show they check 40+ times.
    • Example (Judo/Walking): When black-belt judo players explain how they throw an opponent, their verbal descriptions contradict their actual muscle movement patterns. ➔ Designer rule: Watch users, don't just ask them.
  • Personas: Rich archetypes of fictional users based on real user research data. They help designers ask "How would this specific person react?" instead of "How would a generic user react?"
    • Persona Example: Mahnoor (21-year-old student): Uses phone 6+ hours/day, short attention span, studies at night, gets anxious about deadlines, prefers visual cues over text, and shares notes in WhatsApp groups.
  • Scenarios: Linear stories describing a realistic situation of system use.
    • Example: "Saad Jamil is hungry at 1 AM. He opens the app, searches for a burger, filters by price, and places his order within 2 minutes."
    • Limitations: Scenarios are linear; they ignore alternative paths, connection errors, and user slips. E.g. what happens if Saad's payment gets declined or internet drops?

5.3 Local & Global Navigation Structure

Interaction happens at multiple levels: Widgets (buttons), Screens, Navigation within an app (moving between screens), and the broader environment (switching between apps).

Local vs. Global Structure
• Local Structure: Organization of elements on a single page (e.g. YouTube video window showing like buttons, comments, share widgets).
• Global Structure: Overall organization showing how pages or device states connect (e.g. Netflix sidebar routing between Home, TV Shows, and Movies).
  • Four Key Questions in Local Structure: Every page layout must enable the user to answer:
    1. Where am I? (Solved via page titles, active menu highlights, and breadcrumbs: Home > Electronics > Laptops).
    2. What can I do? (Buttons look clickable, links have underlines, heart icons show tap interactions).
    3. Where will this action take me? (Clear button labels like "Confirm Ride" in Yango).
    4. Where have I been? (Back buttons, browser history, recently visited pages to prevent the user from getting "Lost in Hyperspace").
  • Deep vs. Broad Hierarchies:
    • Deep Hierarchy: Many menu layers ($Menu \rightarrow Category \rightarrow Subcategory \rightarrow Sub-subcategory \rightarrow Item$). Users get lost easily.
    • Broad Hierarchy: Fewer levels with more options per level. Easier to scan quickly.
  • Miller's Magic Number Misuse: Designers often restrict menu items to $7 \pm 2$ because of Miller's short-term memory rule. This is a mistake. Miller's rule applies to *working memory*, not visual menus. A menu can contain 40+ items if they are visually organized and structured (like Wikipedia's main directory).

5.4 Screen Layout & Visual Design Rules

Screen design must support the user's task flow using visual layout tools: grouping, order, alignment, and white space.

Screen Design Rule
1. Ask: What is the user trying to do?
2. Think: What information does the user need? What order should tasks follow?
3. Design: Form follows function. The layout must support the task sequence. (e.g. Yango places the destination field first because the goal is booking a ride).
  • Visual Layout Tools:
    • Grouping & Structure: Physical proximity indicates functional relationship (e.g. grouping billing details, delivery details, and order items into distinct boxes on a checkout screen).
    • Order of Information: Match the natural sequence of user actions. The form tab key navigation order must exactly match the visual top-to-bottom layout of input fields.
    • Alignment: Left-align text lists for readability (left-to-right reading direction). Right-align integers and align decimal numbers at the decimal point to support rapid numerical size comparison.
    • White Space: Creates separation, structure, and highlights critical content by drawing focus. Busy backgrounds reduce accessibility and cause severe eye strain.
  • Appropriate Appearance: The format must match the data type. Numbers should be shown in charts or graphs, comparisons in tables, and hierarchies in tree diagrams. 3D visuals should *only* be used for spatial simulations; using 3D for pie charts distorts user data perception.
  • Localization & Internationalization: Adapting UIs for global regions:
    • Layout Direction: Adjust menu layouts based on reading direction (Left-to-Right for English, Right-to-Left for Arabic/Urdu).
    • Cultural Symbols: The meaning of icons changes. While a "tick" ($\checkmark$) means correct/yes and "cross" ($\text{X}$) means wrong/no in Anglo-American contexts, in some European grading systems, teachers use a cross ($\text{X}$) to mark a correct answer.

Possible Exam Questions with Solutions

Q1: Why is it a bad design practice to limit visual menu lists to exactly 7 items under the pretext of respecting "Miller's Magic Number"?

Answer: Limiting visual menus to 7 items represents a fundamental misuse of Miller's $7 \pm 2$ rule. Miller's rule applies strictly to the capacity limit of *human working memory* when recalling items *without visual aids*. In a user interface, menu options are continuously visible on the screen, meaning the user does not need to memorize them—they rely on **recognition rather than recall**. Limiting menus to 7 items forces designers to build deep hierarchies, which increases navigation clicks, hide options, and increases the likelihood of users getting lost. Visual menus can easily contain dozens of items, provided they are structured, aligned, and categorized properly.

Q2: Describe the "Self-Reporting Trap" in user research and give a concrete example from the uploaded slides showing why it occurs.

Answer: The self-reporting trap is the phenomenon where what users *say* they do in interviews does not match what they *actually* do in practice. This occurs because human actions often become compiled and automatic (proceduralized), making them unconscious. For example, the slides note that when professional judo players or people walking are asked to describe the exact physics and sequence of their movements, their descriptions are incorrect. In digital systems, a student may report checking their phone only 10 times a day, but system logs reveal they check it 40+ times. Therefore, designers must use observational techniques (watching actual usage) rather than relying solely on interviews or questionnaires.

Chapter 6: HCI in the Software Process

Integrating usability goals into software engineering life cycles, building prototypes, and recording design rationales.

6.1 Software Life Cycle & Verification vs. Validation

Software Engineering provides structured processes to design, build, and maintain software. HCI is not a separate step but must influence every stage of this life cycle.

Waterfall Model Stages
1. Requirements Specification: Captures what the system is expected to provide (users, tasks, environment).
2. Architectural Design: High-level system structuring into major components.
3. Detailed Design: Refines components into concrete algorithms and data structures.
4. Coding & Unit Testing: Code implementation and individual module testing.
5. Integration & Testing: Combining modules to test the system as a whole (includes user acceptance testing).
6. Operation & Maintenance: System release, bug fixes, and feature updates.
  • Activities vs. Phases:
    • Activity (Technical perspective): The actual work done (e.g. designing, coding, testing).
    • Phase (Managerial perspective): Defined by structured inputs (documents) and outputs (deliverables), ending in a client **sign-off** (legal/contractual approval).
  • Verification vs. Validation (The Formality Gap):
    • Verification (Building the product right): Checks if the system conforms to its technical specifications. It is objective, mathematically verifiable, and consistent (e.g., "Does the code compile? Does the login reject wrong passwords?").
    • Validation (Building the right product): Checks if the system actually meets the user's real-world needs and expectations. It is subjective and relies on user evaluation (e.g., "Do users find the navigation intuitive?").
    • The Formality Gap: The gap between the formal, mathematical verification of software and the subjective, psychological validation of the user experience. You can mathematically verify that a system works perfectly while failing validation because the UI is unusable.

6.2 Usability Engineering & ISO Usability Standards

Usability engineering, pioneered by industry researchers at DEC and IBM, argues that usability must be explicitly defined, measured, and engineered *before* the system is built, rather than guessed at the end.

6 Elements of a Usability Specification
1. Measuring Concept: The usability attribute to be tested (e.g., recoverability).
2. Measuring Method: How the attribute is measured (e.g., counting steps to undo an error).
3. Now Level: The performance level of existing systems (e.g., zero support/must restart).
4. Worst Case: The minimum acceptable usability level (e.g., takes 6 steps to fix mistake).
5. Planned Level: The target usability goal for designers (e.g., maximum 2 steps).
6. Best Case: The ideal, state-of-the-art scenario (e.g., 1-click undo).
Case Study: VCR Backward Recoverability Spec
Old VCR systems were notoriously hard to program and had no undo mechanism—if a user made one mistake, they had to restart the whole sequence from scratch (Now Level).
Usability Spec for new VCR:
• Attribute: Backward recoverability (undoing a programming error).
• Method: Number of explicit user actions required to correct a mistake.
• Worst Case: As many actions as it takes to program the mistake again.
• Planned Level: A maximum of two actions (click Back ➔ confirm).
• Best Case: One explicit cancel/undo action.
ISO Usability Standard 9241
The **ISO 9241** standard defines usability through three key pillars:
• Effectiveness: Can users successfully achieve their goals? (Accuracy and task completion rate).
• Efficiency: How much time and physical/mental effort does it take? (Speed and resource expenditure).
• Satisfaction: How does the user feel while using it? (Comfort, rating scales, enjoyment).
Usability Objective Effectiveness Measures Efficiency Measures Satisfaction Measures
Suitability for the Task Percentage of goals achieved Time to complete a task Rating scale for task satisfaction
Appropriate for Trained Users Number of power features used Relative efficiency compared with expert Rating scale for power feature satisfaction
Learnability Percentage of functions learned Time to learn criteria Rating scale for ease of learning
Error Tolerance Percentage of errors corrected successfully Time spent on correcting errors Rating scale for system error handling

6.3 Prototyping & Iterative Design

Because human behavior is complex and requirements cannot be fully known at the start, designers build prototypes—early drafts of a system—to test ideas quickly.

Three Prototyping Models
• Throw-away Prototyping: The prototype is built, tested with users to gather requirements or clarify ideas, and then *completely discarded*. The final system is rebuilt from scratch using the insights gained.
• Incremental Prototyping: The system is partitioned into independent functional modules. Each module is prototyped, tested, and added piece-by-piece to form the final combined system.
• Evolutionary Prototyping: The prototype is not discarded. It starts simple, and is continuously tested, refined, and expanded with new features until it evolves into the final production system.
  • Prototyping Techniques:
    • Storyboards: Visual snapshots showing a user flow step-by-step. They have no code or functionality, only demonstrating appearance. Modern storyboards can include basic animations.
    • Limited Functionality Simulation: Partially working interactive mockups (e.g. clicking a "Like" button changes the icon color, but the database backend isn't connected).
  • Management Challenges: Prototyping takes time, cost estimation is difficult, and throw-away prototypes may feel like "wasted effort." Furthermore, prototypes often ignore critical non-functional requirements like security, performance under load, and reliability.

6.4 Design Rationale, IBIS, and QOC Notations

Design rationale is the documentation of *why* a system is designed the way it is, recording the trade-offs, alternative choices, and justifications.

Types of Design Rationale
• Process-Oriented: Records the chronological history of decisions (e.g., first chose burger menu, users got confused, switched to bottom bar).
• Structure-Oriented: Maps all possible design choices as a static space (e.g. QOC notation).
• Psychological: Focuses on user tasks and experiences (task-artefact cycle), making explicit the consequences of design choices for the user.
  • IBIS (Issue-Based Information System): A 1970s process-oriented notation to structure design discussions:
    • Issue: The design problem or question (e.g. "Should we support dark mode?").
    • Position: A possible solution (e.g. "Yes" or "No").
    • Argument: Supporting or opposing points for a position (e.g. "Yes reduces eye strain" ➔ supports; "No increases design complexity" ➔ opposes).
    • gIBIS: The graphical version showing issues, positions, arguments, and sub-issues as a linked tree diagram.
  • QOC (Questions, Options, Criteria) Notation: A structure-oriented notation mapping the design space post-hoc:
    • Questions (Q): The design problems (e.g., "How to handle infinite feed loading?").
    • Options (O): Alternative solutions (e.g., "Infinite Scroll", "Pagination", "Load More Button").
    • Criteria (C): Standard metrics used to evaluate options (e.g., "Ease of use", "Engagement", "User control").
    • Mapping: Options are linked to Criteria with **solid lines** (positive support/good match) or **dashed lines** (negative support/poor match). A highlighted box denotes the chosen option.

Exam Tip: Limitations of QOC

Students often mistake QOC for a decision generator. In reality, QOC has strict limitations:
1. It does *not* show which criterion is more important (no weights).
2. It does *not* always yield a single "winner" option.
3. It serves to document and compare design alternatives, not to dynamically decide for the team.

7.2 Usability Principles (Learnability, Flexibility, Robustness)

HCI groups usability requirements into three overarching categories. Within these are 14 precise sub-principles that dictate interface behavior.

1. Learnability (Ease of Learning for Novices)
  • Predictability: Determining the effect of future actions based on past interaction history. Uses operation visibility: always showing what actions are possible (e.g., play/pause controls in Spotify are always visible, so users don't need to guess).
  • Synthesizability: Assessing the effect of past actions.
    • Immediate Honesty: Instant visual feedback showing state change (e.g., seeing a file move into a folder right away).
    • Eventual Honesty: Delayed feedback (common in command-line systems where users must type ls to check if a file was moved).
  • Familiarity: How prior real-world knowledge applies to the new system (e.g., using a "shopping cart" icon in Amazon). Uses **affordances**—visual clues suggesting how to use an object (e.g., buttons look raised/clickable; sliders look draggable).
  • Generalizability: Extending specific interaction knowledge to new situations (e.g. knowing that swiping up works in TikTok, and successfully applying it to Instagram Reels).
  • Consistency: Likeness in input/output behavior in similar contexts (e.g., the heart icon means "like" across Facebook and Instagram).
    ⚠️ When Consistency Fails: Too much consistency can be counterproductive. E.g., old alphabetical keyboards are logical, but are highly inefficient compared to the standardized QWERTY layout.
2. Flexibility (Multiplicity of Ways to Exchange Info)
  • Dialogue Initiative: Who controls the interaction flow?
    • System-driven (Pre-emptive): The system forces the user (e.g. modal popups blocking all other actions).
    • User-driven: The user controls the navigation freely (e.g. browsing YouTube).
    • Restriction context: System control is needed for safety or collaboration (e.g. Google Docs locking a paragraph while someone else edits to prevent conflicts).
  • Multithreading: Supporting interaction for more than one task at a time. Can be concurrent (tasks active simultaneously, like typing while listening to music) or interleaved (switching rapidly between tasks, like swiping between chat windows).
  • Task Migratability: Passing responsibility for task execution between the user and the system (e.g., Google Maps suggests a route based on traffic; the user can accept it or manually override and select an alternative route).
  • Substitutivity: Allowing equivalent input and output values to be substituted for each other.
    • Representation Multiplicity: Showing the same data in different formats (e.g., Google Sheets showing numbers in a table OR a visual bar graph).
    • Equal Opportunity: Blurring the line between inputs and outputs (e.g. dragging a file out of search results ➔ the output screen element is used directly as an input selector).
  • Customizability: Modifiability of the UI by the user (Adaptability, like setting chat wallpapers) or by the system (Adaptivity, like AI-driven recommendations in Netflix).
3. Robustness (Support for Goal Achievement)
  • Observability: The user's ability to evaluate the internal system state. Includes:
    • Browsability: Ability to explore the system state without changing it (scrolling).
    • Defaults: Pre-filled inputs reducing user effort.
    • Reachability: Can the user navigate anywhere? (e.g., home button prevents getting stuck).
    • Persistence: Information remains visible (e.g. unread count badges).
  • Recoverability: Ability of the user to take corrective action once an error occurs.
    • Backward Recovery: Undoing a mistake (Ctrl + Z).
    • Forward Recovery: Fixing the error by moving forward (e.g. auto-correcting a typo).
    • Commensurate Effort: Actions that are hard to undo (like account deletion) must be hard to do (require multiple confirmation steps) to prevent accidental loss.
  • Responsiveness: How the user perceives the speed of communication (systems must respond quickly; lag causes abandonment).
  • Task Conformance: The degree to which system services support all user tasks.
    • Task Completeness: Can the system do the whole task?
    • Task Adequacy: Does the system do it in a way the user understands?

7.3 Shneiderman's 8 Golden Rules & Norman's 7 Principles

These represent simple, practical design checklists compiled by leading HCI researchers to ensure usability in production interfaces.

Shneiderman's 8 Golden Rules
1. Strive for consistency: Keep layouts, icons, and menus consistent.
2. Enable frequent users to use shortcuts: Provide macros and hotkeys (Ctrl + C).
3. Offer informative feedback: Show typing indicators ("...") or success prompts.
4. Design dialogs to yield closure: Signal task completion clearly ("Order placed successfully").
5. Offer error prevention & simple error handling: Prevent inputs that cause errors (e.g. disabling a button if a form field is empty).
6. Permit easy reversal of actions: Provide simple undo functions.
7. Support internal locus of control: Make the user feel in control of the system, not vice versa (e.g. clear skip and seek controls).
8. Reduce short-term memory load: Rely on recognition, not recall (use auto-suggestions).
Norman's 7 Principles for Usability
1. Use both knowledge in the world and knowledge in the head: Provide visible cues (world) so users don't rely only on memory (head).
2. Simplify the structure of tasks: Break complex workflows into simple steps (e.g. Uber's 3-click booking).
3. Make things visible: Bridge the Gulf of Execution and Gulf of Evaluation.
4. Get the mappings right: Ensure physical controls match logical outcomes (e.g., swiping left moves to the next card).
5. Exploit the power of constraints: Prevent users from making mistakes (e.g., graying out unavailable slots).
6. Design for error: Assume mistakes *will* happen. Provide trash folders and undo paths.
7. Standardize: If all else fails, use industry-standard design patterns (e.g., standard play/pause icons).
  • HCI Design Patterns: Reusable design solutions to recurring problems in specific contexts. Just like architectural patterns (e.g., having windows on two sides of a room to let in natural light), UI patterns solve visual disorientation (e.g. placing breadcrumb trails at the top of e-commerce pages so users always know their location).

Q1: A mobile application requires users to complete a 5-step registration. If the connection fails at Step 4, the app redirects to the login screen and deletes all inputs, forcing the user to re-enter everything. Evaluate this behavior using the sub-principles of Robustness.

Answer: This behavior represents a catastrophic failure of **Recoverability** and **Task Conformance**. Under Recoverability, the system must allow the user to take corrective action once an error occurs (like connection failure) with **Commensurate Effort**. Forcing the user to re-enter all fields (high effort) to recover from a system-side connection drop (which was not the user's fault) violates the principle of commensurate effort. Furthermore, the lack of cached data state violates **Observability** (specifically, persistence of state). To conform to robustness principles, the app should use forward recovery: preserve the inputs of steps 1-3 in local storage and display a "Retry connection" button on Step 4, keeping the user in control.

Q2: Differentiate between the terms "Synthesizability" and "Predictability" under the Learnability category. Provide a real-world software example for each.

Answer:
• **Predictability** is a forward-looking sub-principle. It refers to the user's ability to determine what the effect of a *future* action will be, based on past interaction history and visual cues (operation visibility). Example: On a media player, seeing a distinct triangle icon represents "Play", allowing the user to predict that clicking it will start the video.
• **Synthesizability** is a backward-looking sub-principle. It refers to the user's ability to assess the effect of a *past* action (evaluating what just happened). It relies on immediate system honesty (feedback). Example: In a database app, after clicking "Save Record", a banner reading "Record saved successfully" immediately confirms the system state update. Without this feedback (eventual honesty), the user is left unsure if the save succeeded.

Chapter 8: Implementation Support

Architectural systems, windowing models, event languages, User Interface Management Systems (UIMS), and structural design architectures.

8.1 Windowing System Elements & Architectures

Windowing systems provide the core infrastructure supporting WIMP interfaces, sitting between application programs and the raw hardware drivers.

Device Independence
• Device Independence: Hides hardware differences (e.g., mouse vs. touchscreen inputs, display resolution variations) so programmers don't code for each device separately.
• Abstract Terminal Concept: A virtual device model. The programmer writes code to this abstract terminal, which translates commands into machine-specific instructions (e.g. Java virtual machine).
• Imaging Model: Defines how graphics are displayed using drawing primitives (shapes, text, visual layouts).
  • Three Windowing System Architectures:
    • 1. Application-Managed (Inside Application): Each application manages its own input/output, multitasking, and resource conflicts. This results in massive programming complexity, repeated development effort, and poor application portability.
    • 2. OS Kernel-Managed (Inside Operating System): Window management is handled directly inside the OS kernel. This yields centralized control and high efficiency, but ties applications strictly to a single operating system (e.g., Windows apps cannot run on Linux).
    • 3. Client-Server Model (Separate System): The windowing system runs as a separate program (the server), while applications behave as clients connecting via standard network protocols (e.g., the X Window System / X11). This yields maximum portability and allows running apps remotely.
      • X Client: The application itself, which sends draw requests and handles logical input events.
      • X Server: Manages the local screen display, captures keyboard/mouse inputs, distributes events, and controls screen rendering.

8.2 Programming the App: Control Flow Paradigms

Developers use two primary paradigms to manage how user actions (events) trigger software responses.

Games built in Unity or custom graphics engines containing active update loops.
Notification-Based (Event-Driven) A central notifier (part of the toolkit) controls the loop. The app registers callback functions for specific events. 8.4 Component Architectures: MVC vs. PAC

Modern interactive systems avoid monolithic Seeheim structures, utilizing component-based architectures where independent modules handle specific concerns.

MVC (Model-View-Controller)
• Model: Encapsulates data and logic (independent of UI). E.g. user profile databases in Instagram.
• View: Handles output representation, displaying model data to the user. E.g. rendering a grid of photos.
• Controller: Handles user input and updates the Model or View. E.g. tapping "Like" sends an event to the database.

⚠️ The MVC Input Issue: Input (e.g. mouse click coordinates) is meaningless in isolation from what is displayed on the screen. The Controller must communicate closely with the View to know *what* was clicked, meaning the separation of concerns is not completely separate.
PAC (Presentation-Abstraction-Control) Model
PAC breaks the system into a tree hierarchy of small, cooperating, semi-autonomous agents (e.g. main screen, sidebar, and chat window are separate PAC units). Each PAC unit has:
• Presentation: Handles both input and output combined (unlike MVC view).
• Abstraction: Stores the state and business logic.
• Control: Mediates between presentation and abstraction within the agent, and manages communication with other agents.
  • PAC vs. MVC Comparison:
    • Why MVC is more popular: It is simpler to understand, easier to implement, and widely supported by frameworks (Django, ASP.NET).
    • Why PAC is cleaner: It enforces clear responsibilities with an explicit Control mediator layer. It is highly scalable for complex systems with multiple views, as local agent changes don't crash the global system.

8.5 Dialog Specification & Notations

Developers use different notations to program the dialog connections between the presentation and application layers.

  • Menu Networks: Dialog is represented as a structured tree of screens, menus, and actions (e.g. HyperCard). Clicking a visual element moves to another "card". Good for structured navigation, but rigid and poor for complex interactions.
  • Grammar Notations: Model dialogue like a language. Good for command-based systems (CLIs), but poor for graphical, direct-manipulation UIs.
  • State Transition Diagrams: Graphical charts tracking system states and transition triggers (e.g. Login page ➔ click Login ➔ validating credentials ➔ Success or Error screen). Hard to scale for large systems due to state explosion.
  • Event Languages: Code based on production rules (e.g., "If $input = invalid\_email$ then show error message"). Good for validation.
  • Declarative Languages: Focus on *what* the relationship should be rather than *how* it happens in sequence.
    • Example: $Total\_Price = Quantity \times Price$. No trigger events (clicks) are explicitly written; the system maintains the constraint automatically.
  • Constraints: Restrict variables to preserve logical connections (e.g., $Displayed\_Balance = Actual\_Account\_Balance$. Sending money updates both variables instantly).

Q1: Sketch the logical flow of the Seeheim Model and explain the role of the "Bypass Switch" when dragging a file icon over the "Trash" icon on a desktop interface.

Answer: The Seeheim Model consists of: Presentation (renders UI elements) ➔ Dialog Control (handles dialogue syntax) ➔ Application Interface (semantic logic).
When a user drags a file icon, the cursor position is processed at the lexical (Presentation) level. If the file is dragged directly over the "Trash" icon, the system needs to evaluate whether a semantic relationship exists: "Is the file over the Trash? Should the Trash highlight and open its lid?" Normally, this requires traversing through the Dialog Control layer to the Application layer and back, causing visual latency. To prevent this, the **Bypass Switch** creates a direct link between the Presentation and Application components. The Application instantly calculates that the file coordinates overlap the Trash coordinates (Semantic Feedback) and triggers the Presentation layer to open the Trash lid icon instantly, bypassing the dialogue syntax parser.

Q2: Why does the MVC View require close communication with the Controller, and how does the PAC model attempt to resolve this issue?

Answer: In MVC, the Controller is conceptually responsible for handling inputs, and the View handles outputs. However, in graphical interfaces, inputs only have meaning relative to the layout. For example, a mouse click at coordinates (x=100, y=150) is meaningless unless the Controller knows what UI element is rendered at that exact spot. Since the layout is stored inside the View, the Controller must ask the View where elements are located. This breaks the strict separation of concerns. The PAC model resolves this by combining input and output inside the **Presentation** component of each individual agent. The agent's **Control** component mediates this relationship internally, preventing the controller from needing to query external presentation models directly.

Chapter 9: Evaluation Techniques

Comparing design prototypes through cognitive walkthroughs, heuristic reviews, usability testing, and physiological metrics.

9.1 What is Evaluation & Its Goals

Evaluation is the process of assessing the usability and functionality of an interactive system. It is a continuous process that occurs throughout the design cycle, not just at the end.

Three Goals of Evaluation
1. Assess System Functionality: Ensure the system supports user tasks and has easy-to-access features.
2. Assess User Experience: Evaluate how easy the system is to learn, its usability, and the user's satisfaction, cognitive load, and emotional response.
3. Identify Specific Problems: Uncover confusing parts of the UI, unexpected behavior, and user error hotspots.
  • Evaluation Paradigms:
    • Expert-Based Evaluation: Performed by designers or usability experts without real users. Best for early design stages and quick, cost-effective feedback.
    • User-Based Evaluation: Involves testing with real users to observe actual behavior, errors, and emotional reactions.

9.2 Expert Analysis (Walkthroughs, Heuristics, Models)

Expert evaluation uses psychological theories, design rules of thumb, and cognitive models to identify usability issues without requiring user testing.

1. Cognitive Walkthrough (4 Key Questions)
A step-by-step task evaluation focusing on ease of learning by exploration. The expert simulates the user's actions and asks:
1. Goal Match: Will the user try to achieve the correct effect? ("Is this what I want to do?")
2. Visibility: Will the user notice that the correct action is available? ("Can I find it?")
3. Recognition: Will the user associate the correct action with the desired effect? ("Is this the right button?")
4. Feedback: If the correct action is performed, will the user see that progress is being made? ("Did it work?")
2. Heuristic Evaluation (Nielsen & Molich)
System is checked against Nielsen's 10 Usability Heuristics. Usability problems are assigned a Severity Rating (0–4):
• 0: I don’t agree that this is a usability problem at all.
• 1 (Cosmetic): Need not be fixed unless extra time is available.
• 2 (Minor): Low priority.
• 3 (Major): High priority to fix.
• 4 (Usability Catastrophe): Imperative to fix before release.
Severity factors: Frequency, impact, persistence, and ease of fixing.
Heuristic Description HCI Example
1. Visibility of system status Always keep users informed about what is happening through appropriate feedback. Displaying a file upload progress bar.
2. Match between system and real world Use user-familiar language, phrases, and concepts rather than technical jargon. Labeling a checkout button "Add to Cart" instead of "Execute transaction_id".
3. User control and freedom Provide an emergency exit to undo or cancel accidental actions. "Undo Send" popup in Gmail.
4. Consistency and standards Follow established platform conventions so users don't wonder if words mean the same. Placing settings in the top-right corner.
5. Error prevention Eliminate error-prone conditions or check for them before actions are executed. Disabling the "Submit" button until the email field is valid.

7. Flexibility and efficiency of use Provide accelerators (shortcuts) that are invisible to novices but speed up expert tasks. Supporting keyboard shortcuts like Ctrl + C.
8. Aesthetic and minimalist design Do not clutter screens with irrelevant information. Keep it clean. The minimalist home page of Google Search.

    • GOMS (Goals, Operators, Methods, Selection): Predicts task performance sequences by breaking user tasks into goals (what to do) and operators (basic clicks/keypresses).

  • Recommended Expert Evaluation Order:
    1. Heuristic Evaluation: Fix obvious, low-level UI layout issues.
    2. Cognitive Walkthrough: Optimize learnability and step-by-step task flows.
    3. Model-Based Evaluation: Refine keystroke performance and speed for expert users.

9.3 User-Based & Experimental Evaluation

Testing with real users reveals actual behavior and emotional reactions that experts might miss.

Lab vs. Field Studies
• Laboratory Studies (Controlled): Conducted in a dedicated usability lab with no distractions, using specialized recording tools (eye-tracking, screen recorders). Pros: accurate metrics. Cons: artificial environment doesn't reflect real-life stress.
• Field Studies (Real Environment): Conducted in the user's natural environment (office, home). Pros: captures real behavior, multitasking, and interruptions. Cons: noisy, hard to control variables.
  • Controlled Experiments: Tests hypotheses by manipulating variables.
    • Independent Variable (IV): The factor manipulated by the evaluator (e.g. menu layout style, font size, button color).
    • Dependent Variable (DV): The factor measured to evaluate usability (e.g. task completion time, error rates, clicks).
    • Hypothesis: Prediction of outcome (e.g. "fewer menu items lead to lower error rates"). The Null Hypothesis predicts "no difference between layouts." The goal of the experiment is to disprove the null hypothesis.
    • Experimental Design Types:
      - Between-Subjects: Different users test different layouts (Group 1 tests Layout A, Group 2 tests Layout B). Pros: no learning bias. Cons: requires more users, and individual differences can skew results.
      - Within-Subjects: The same users test all layouts. Pros: requires fewer users, highly consistent. Cons: prone to **learning effects** (users perform better on the second layout simply because they got used to the system).
  • Sample Size Rules of Thumb:
    • **5 users:** Identifies 85% of core usability issues (cost-effective).
    • **10+ users:** Required for statistically significant experimental data.

9.4 Observational, Query, and Physiological Methods

Evaluators choose from a matrix of subjective, objective, qualitative, and quantitative methods based on resources and objectives.

  • Observational Techniques:
    • Think Aloud: The user talks aloud, explaining what they are doing and why. Pros: simple, reveals the user's thinking. Cons: subjective, obtrusive, slows user speed (changes behavior).
    • Cooperative Evaluation: User and evaluator collaborate, asking each other clarifying questions.
    • Protocol Analysis: Recording session logs. Methods: paper notes, audio, video, keypress logging, user diaries. Major challenge: Data overload and synchronization issues.
    • Post-Task Walkthrough: Replaying the recorded session to the user afterward, asking them to explain why they made specific errors. Crucial for silent users.
  • Query Techniques (Asking Users):
    • Interviews: One-on-one, flexible discussions. Pros: deep insights. Cons: subjective, time-consuming.
    • Questionnaires: Structured questions (scalar ratings, MCQs, open-ended) sent to many users. Pros: quick, cheap, easy statistical analysis. Cons: less probing.
  • Physiological Evaluation Methods (Objective Body Signals):
    • Eye Tracking: Tracks eye fixations (where focus lies), fixation duration (longer duration = confusion), and scan path. Reveals if users miss key buttons.
    • Physiological Measurements: Heart rate (stress/anger), sweat/Galvanic Skin Response (GSR - effort/stress), muscle activity (EMG - engagement), and brain activity (EEG - attention). Pros: objective, un-biased, detects hidden emotions. Cons: expensive, special labs, same signal can have multiple meanings.
  • CSCW Groupware Evaluation Challenges: Much more complex than single-user testing. Human-human group dynamics vary (one group is active, another has conflicts, a third has a silent leader, yielding different outcomes on the same system). Group testing requires 3x more participants, time-critical tasks, and multi-POV video synchronization.

Quick Revision Summary

  • Cognitive Walkthrough: Step-by-step, focuses on ease of learning via 4 questions.
  • Heuristic Evaluation: Checked against 10 rules, rated on 0-4 severity scale.
  • Laboratory: Controlled, high precision, artificial. Field: Real context, noise.
  • Between-subjects: Layout A/B tested on different groups. No learning effect.
  • Within-subjects: Same users test all layouts. Prone to learning bias.
  • Think Aloud: Obtrusive but simple qualitative user observation.
  • Physiological metrics: GSR, heart rate, and eye tracking yield objective data.
Possible Exam Questions with Solutions

Q1: You are designing a usability test to compare a new gesture-based swiping menu (Layout A) with a traditional tap-based grid menu (Layout B). Choose between Within-Subjects and Between-Subjects designs, state your Independent and Dependent variables, and justify your choice.

Answer:
• **Design Choice:** Between-Subjects Design.
• **Justification:** Gesture-based navigation (swiping) requires motor learning. If we used a Within-Subjects design, users testing the gesture layout first would carry over their learning and familiarity to the grid menu, or vice versa, creating a severe **learning effect** (bias). A Between-Subjects design ensures each user starts fresh, preventing carry-over bias.
• **Independent Variable (IV):** The menu navigation style (Gesture-based Layout A vs. Traditional Grid Layout B).
• **Dependent Variables (DVs):** 1. Task Completion Time (seconds), 2. Number of navigation errors/slips, 3. Satisfaction rating (measured via a post-test questionnaire).

Q2: Detail the four key questions an evaluator must answer at every step of a Cognitive Walkthrough, and apply them to a user trying to "Delete a chat history" in a messaging app.

Answer:
• **1. Goal Match:** Will the user associate the system's action path with their goal? (e.g. does the user know they need to tap and hold the chat bubble to access deletion options?).
• **2. Visibility:** Is the control element visible? (e.g. is the trash icon visible on the action bar, or is it hidden in a submenu?).
• **3. Recognition:** Will the user recognize the control as the correct option? (e.g. does the trash icon clearly mean "delete chat," or could it be confused with "archive"?).
• **4. Feedback:** Is there clear, immediate system honesty after execution? (e.g. does a popup read "Chat history deleted" immediately after tapping, or does the screen freeze without confirmation?).

Exam Scenario 1: SCADA System Design and Feedback Lag

Question: An industrial plant operator controls a remote high-voltage power breaker via a graphical SCADA screen. Due to network latency, a breaker switch shows "Closed" on the screen immediately upon clicking, but the actual physical breaker takes 4 seconds to close. Assess this design using Abowd & Beale's framework and identify the critical hazard.

Answer:
Under the **Abowd & Beale framework**, the interaction sequence is:
1. **Articulation:** The user clicks the switch icon (translating Task Language "close breaker" into Input Language "mouse click").
2. **Performance:** The input system updates the SCADA screen state (Input Language ➔ Core Language).
3. **Presentation:** The SCADA screen immediately updates the switch icon color to green / label to "Closed" (Core Language ➔ Output Language).
4. **Observation:** The user perceives the "Closed" status and assumes the system state matches their goal.

The Hazard (The Presentation Mismatch): The system provided **syntactic honesty** (the click was registered and icon updated) but was **semantically dishonest**. The actual core system state (the physical breaker at the plant) was still "Open" during the 4-second delay. If the operator acts immediately on this false screen confirmation, it could result in electrocution or equipment failure. The design must be corrected to show a transit state (e.g. "Closing...") and only display "Closed" once the physical feedback sensors confirm the breaker has fully engaged.

Exam Scenario 2: Evaluation Method Selection

Question: A startup has developed a prototype for a mobile learning app. They have a limited budget and need to identify obvious UI issues and first-time user navigation errors before coding the final release. State which evaluation methods should be applied, the order of application, and justify your choice.

Answer:
The startup should apply expert-based evaluation methods in the following order:
1. Heuristic Evaluation (First): Evaluators inspect the prototype against Nielsen's 10 Heuristics to find low-level layout mistakes, color issues, and menu standardizations.
Justification: This is cheap, fast, and removes obvious "noise" problems so that subsequent tests can focus on task flows.
2. Cognitive Walkthrough (Second): Evaluators simulate step-by-step user tasks (e.g. booking a lesson) using the 4 key questions to test first-time learnability.
Justification: Walkthroughs specifically focus on first-time user exploration and detect where menu labels or missing feedback block goal achievement.
3. Questionnaire / Survey (Third): After resolving obvious issues, they can conduct a limited user test with 5–10 students and gather qualitative satisfaction ratings using a standard questionnaire.
Justification: Real users reveal unexpected behaviors that experts miss, and questionnaires are a low-cost query technique suitable for tight startup budgets.

Exam Scenario 3: QOC Analysis for Menu Structures

Question: Draw a text-based QOC (Questions, Options, Criteria) mapping for the design question: "How should a smartphone application show its navigation options?" Compare two options: (A) Tab bar at bottom, and (B) Hidden drawer (hamburger menu) at top-left, against three criteria: (1) Screen space efficiency, (2) Ease of physical reach, and (3) Visibility of options.

Answer:
• **Question (Q):** How should a smartphone application show its navigation options?
• **Option 1 (O1):** Tab bar at bottom.
• **Option 2 (O2):** Hidden drawer (hamburger menu) at top-left.
• **Criterion 1 (C1):** Screen space efficiency.
• **Criterion 2 (C2):** Ease of physical reach (one-handed phone usage).
• **Criterion 3 (C3):** Visibility of options (recognition over recall).

QOC Mapping Relationships:
• **O1 (Tab Bar) connects to:**
    - **C1 (Space):** *Negative relationship* (dashed line) because the tab bar consumes permanent vertical screen space.
    - **C2 (Reach):** *Positive relationship* (solid line) because the bottom zone is within easy reach of the user's thumb.
    - **C3 (Visibility):** *Positive relationship* (solid line) because options are continuously visible to the user.
• **O2 (Hidden Drawer) connects to:**
    - **C1 (Space):** *Positive relationship* (solid line) because it remains hidden, maximizing screen space.
    - **C2 (Reach):** *Negative relationship* (dashed line) because the top-left corner is extremely difficult to reach during one-handed usage.
    - **C3 (Visibility):** *Negative relationship* (dashed line) because options are hidden, forcing users to recall menu structures.
