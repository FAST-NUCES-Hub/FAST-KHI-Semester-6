Topic 01 · Research Report

Research Writing — Prefatory Parts, Results, Discussion & Conclusion

How to plan, structure, and write every part of a technical research report: cover materials, beginning, body, and ending — with focus on what each section must do, the tense and tone it uses, and how Results differs from Discussion.

What a Technical Report Is & Why It Matters

A technical report communicates process, progress, or results. It is objective and factual, written for a specific audience, and exists to support decision making, add to knowledge, and document work.

Research Report

Communicates the results and the procedure of a scientific investigation. Example: AI model performance study.

Project Report

Provides details about a project undertaken to solve a problem. Example: Mobile app development.

Feasibility Report

Tells whether a project is practical or financially & technologically possible. Example: Should we build this startup app?

Recommendation Report

Studies a problem and then makes a recommendation. Example: AWS vs Azure.

Lab Report

Documents lab procedures and their results. Example: Network simulation results.

Progress Report

Communicates progress on ongoing projects. Example: Weekly sprint updates.

Overall structure (memorise): Cover Materials → Beginning Section (Introduction + Literature) → Body (Methodology + Results + Discussion) → Ending Section (Conclusion + Recommendations + References + Appendix).

Prefatory Parts — The Cover Materials

These appear before the main body. They prepare the reader, summarise the work, and let busy decision-makers grasp the report without reading it in full.

1) Title Page

  • Neat and organised.
  • Clearly indicates the subject title in the upper half of the page.
  • The title must specifically identify both the report's function and subject.
  • The writer, the recipient, and the date of submittal appear in the lower half of the page.

2) Letter / Memo of Transmittal

  • Addressed to the official who authorised the report; signed by the official authorised to produce it.
  • States the title and subject of the report.
  • Can include a summary.
  • Can acknowledge those who assisted in preparing the report.
  • Formal tone. Acts as the official acknowledgement of completion and includes a statement of its transmittal.

3) Abstract

Tells the reader what the report contains; gives the gist of the contents; explains the problem and how it was studied; and helps the reader decide whether the report is relevant to their interest.

Standard word limit: 100 – 250 words.

Informative Abstract

Purpose: to fully summarise the entire report.

Includes: Research Problem + Objectives + Methodology + Key Findings + Conclusions.

Tense: mostly present and past (work is completed).

Descriptive Abstract

Purpose: to describe what the report contains, not the results.

Includes: Research Problem + Purpose + Scope + Method (briefly).

Tense: mostly present + future.

4) Executive Summary

What it is: a summary for busy administrators and decision makers who will not have time to read the entire report — and for individuals who lack the required background.

Purpose: not just to inform but to help in decision-making. It helps readers quickly understand the report and decide whether to approve, fund, or take action.

What it should include — a mini version of the entire report:

  • Problem – existing / identified issue
  • Purpose – your aim
  • Method – how
  • Findings – your discovery
  • Conclusions
  • Recommendations – what should be done next
  • Limitations

Introduction — Setting the Stage

The introduction sets the context of the research, defines the problem, reviews existing work briefly, identifies the research gap, and presents the proposed solution. It answers: what is the problem and why does it matter?

Purpose

The overall aim — what you are trying to achieve. Why are you doing this project?

Problem

The specific issue or gap your report addresses. What is not working or missing?

Significance

Why the problem matters; who benefits and how. Why should anyone care?

Scope & Limitation

The boundaries of your work — what is included and what is not. What will (and will not) the project cover?

Structure of a strong introduction

  1. Background context — introduce the domain, define key concepts, move from general to specific. Example: Explain the retina and its function → its role in diagnosis.
  2. Problem — clearly describe the issue. Be specific. Avoid vague statements. Example: Retinal diseases cause blindness; early detection is difficult.
  3. Importance of the problem — why it matters, who is affected, real-world impact. Example: Millions affected globally; early diagnosis prevents vision loss.
  4. Existing solutions (mini literature) — briefly mention current approaches; do not go into full detail. Example: CAD systems, machine learning, deep-learning approaches.
  5. Limitations — identify weaknesses in existing work; this builds justification. Example: Not accurate; focus on a single disease.
  6. Research gap — what is missing? what has not been solved? No gap = no research. Example: Existing models cannot detect multiple diseases simultaneously.
  7. Proposed solution — what are you going to do? Example: Develop a multi-label classification system using a transformer-based model.
Citations in the introduction — when to cite: when you state statistics (factual data), refer to existing research, mention methods/technologies used by others, or make specific research-backed claims. Do not cite your own observations in the problem statement, your research gap (your analysis of literature), or general/common knowledge.

Three quick self-questions to decide if you need a citation:

  • Did I get this from a source? → Cite
  • Is this a research claim or data? → Cite
  • Is this my idea or interpretation? → No citation

Literature Review — Detailed Analysis of Prior Work

A literature review is a section where you analyse, compare, and synthesize existing research to show what has been done and what is still missing. It answers: what has already been studied? what methods have been used? what are the findings? what is still missing?

Introduction vs. Literature Review

Introduction
  • Brief overview
  • Mentions studies
  • Identifies the gap
Literature Review
  • Detailed analysis
  • Explains studies
  • Justifies the gap

Purpose of the Literature Review

  • Show understanding of the field.
  • Identify existing methods.
  • Compare approaches.
  • Highlight limitations.
  • Justify your research.

How to organise paragraphs

In a literature review, paragraphs are usually organised by ideas, not by papers.

  • Thematic organisation — a theme is a common idea, method, or issue across multiple studies.
  • Paragraph pattern:
    • Topic sentence — what is the theme?
    • Synthesis + evidence — support from papers.
  • Conclude the section by highlighting the limitation (what is missing) — this is the justification of your research gap.

Helpful sentence patterns

For synthesis:

  • Several studies have investigated…
  • Recent research focuses on…
  • A number of researchers have explored…

For comparison:

  • In contrast to…
  • However, unlike…
  • While some studies… others…

For gap:

  • However, existing studies do not address…
  • There is limited research on…
  • This highlights the need for…

Body of the Report — Methodology, Results & Discussion

Specification & Design

What exactly are you building, and how will it work?

Implementation

Explain what you did — the methodology.

Results

Present what you produced and achieved.

Evaluation / Discussion

Interpret and analyse the results.

Mental model: what you planned → what you built → what happened → what it means.

Results vs. Discussion — The Critical Distinction

This is one of the most-confused distinctions in research writing. Use these definitions exactly as they appear in the course material.

Results / Findings

What happened?

Presents what you produced and achieved.

Example: "60% of users reported slow loading times."

Discussion / Insights

Why it happened — what it means.

Interprets and analyses the results.

Example: "System performance issues are negatively affecting user experience."

Rule of thumb: Results report raw outcomes; Discussion provides interpretation and meaning. Putting interpretation in Results — or repeating raw numbers in Discussion — is the single most common research-writing mistake.

From Data to Decision — the analytical ladder

  1. Data — raw numbers
  2. Patterns — trends you notice
  3. Insights — explanation of why the patterns exist
  4. Recommendations — action that should be taken

Conclusion, Future Work, References & Appendix

Conclusion

A final summary of your project. Includes: restate purpose, key findings, overall outcome.

Future Work / Recommendations

Suggests what can be improved or done next.

References

A list of all sources cited in the report.

Appendix

Supporting material too detailed for the main body: questionnaire, interview protocol, raw data, screenshots, etc.

Transitioning Between Sections — Creating a Coherent Narrative

The course notes structure a research report as a logical chain in which each section feeds the next. To create a coherent research narrative, ensure that:

  • The Introduction's research gap is the same gap justified in detail by the Literature Review.
  • The Methodology is the practical answer to that gap — it implements the proposed solution announced in the Introduction.
  • Results show what happened when the methodology was carried out — nothing more.
  • Discussion interprets those Results, returning explicitly to the problem and significance stated in the Introduction.
  • Conclusion restates the purpose, summarises key findings, and points forward to future work / recommendations.

In short: Problem → Gap → Method → Results → Meaning → Next steps. Each section must refer back to the previous one.

Common Mistakes

⚠️ Common Mistakes — Research Writing
  • Putting interpretation in the Results section — Results = "what happened?", not "why it happened."
  • Listing papers one by one in the Literature Review instead of organising by theme.
  • Copy-pasting abstracts from source papers into your literature review.
  • No comparison between studies in the literature review.
  • No research gap — without a gap, the project has no academic value.
  • No connection to your own study — the review must justify your project.
  • Citing common knowledge, or failing to cite statistics and prior methods.
  • Vague problem statements — the problem must be specific.
  • Mixing the problem with the proposed solution.
  • Using personal opinion (I, we, you) in a report that should be objective and impersonal.

Topic 02 · Survey Writing

Short Survey Report

An objective document that transforms raw survey data into meaningful, decision-ready insights — written in a formal, impersonal style with percentages and reporting verbs.

Definition & Purpose

A survey report is a document whose task is to present the information gathered during a survey in an objective manner. It presents a summary of all responses in a simple and visually appealing manner.

A survey report is:

  • A formal document presenting collected data.
  • An objective summary of responses.
  • A tool for decision making — answering what was found? what does it mean? what should be done?

The report follows a standard organisation with different sections, headings, and subheadings, and is created at the end of the survey (after constructing and gathering responses).

Survey reports are an integral part of research, and it is critical that you always present accurate data. A survey report is a formal piece of writing based on research.

Why survey reports matter

You encounter surveys in tech and daily digital life — app feedback forms, product reviews, UX feedback forms. Surveys transform raw data into meaningful insights. Companies rely on this data to fix bugs, improve UI, and increase user retention.

Types of Survey Reports

Employee Satisfaction Survey

Gauges whether employees are satisfied with the work environment. Organisations use it to ensure employees are motivated and to build a stronger team spirit. Employees give feedback and voice frustrations with the company.

Customer Feedback Survey

One of the most common survey types. Businesses request feedback after selling a product or rendering a service — collects feedback on products/services and improves user experiences.

Market Research Survey

Discovers customer needs, competitive advantage, how and where products are purchased. Applied when no data is available yet (e.g., measuring how a target audience feels about a planned product) or to build on past research.

Note: For each of the different survey types, the structure of the survey report will be slightly different from the others.

Full Structure of a Survey Report

I. Introduction

State the purpose / aim of the report, when, and how the information was gathered. The introduction also identifies the audience the survey was conducted for.

II. Main Body / Findings

All the information collected and analysed is presented clearly and in detail.

  • Break down respondents into groups according to sex, age, place of residence, etc.
  • State the main differences between groups.
  • Use subheadings, numbers, or letters to separate each piece of information.
  • Findings are data + comparisons.

III. Conclusion

Sums up the points mentioned above — the key insights. If necessary, a recommendation can be included as well; one way of summing up is making some general comments.

IV. Recommendations

Actionable steps based on the findings — what should be done next.

How to Present Survey Data in Writing

When reporting the results of a survey, figures should be given in the form of percentages and proportions.

  • Exact percentages: "25% of the people questioned", "68% of those who filled in the questionnaire".
  • Proportional expressions: "one in four", "six out of ten".
  • Less exact / approximate expressions: "the majority of those questioned", "a large proportion of", "a significant number of", "a small proportion".

Findings vs. Insights — never confuse them

Results / Findings

What happened?

"60% of users reported slow loading times."

Insights

Why it happened?

"System performance issues are negatively affecting user experience."

Tone & Language Conventions

Present tenses, reported speech, and an impersonal style should be used in survey reports.

1) Formal and impersonal tone

Avoid personal opinions (I, we, you). Focus on objectivity and a neutral tone.

❌ WEAK
We think the system is slow.
✅ IMPROVED
The system is considered slow.
What changed: The personal pronoun "we" and the subjective verb "think" were removed and replaced with a passive, impersonal construction that focuses on the system itself — meeting the formal, impersonal tone the report demands.

2) Use of passive voice

Passive voice emphasises the results. Common structures:

  • It was found that…
  • It was observed that…
  • The data was collected…

3) Reporting verbs

Use a variety of formal and precise verbs — claim, state, report, agree, complain, suggest, indicate, demonstrate, reveal.

Example: "The results indicate a high level of dissatisfaction."

4) Data expressions

  • Exact figures: 70%, 25%, 40% of respondents.
  • Approximate expressions: the majority, a significant number, a small proportion.

Example: "The majority of users reported issues with system speed."

5) Useful language for the four moves

  • To introduce: "The purpose / aim of this report…", "As requested…", "This survey was carried out / conducted by means of…", "The questionnaire consisted of…"
  • To generalise: "In general", "Generally", "On the whole"
  • To refer to a fact: "The fact is that…", "In fact", "In practice"
  • To conclude / summarise: "In conclusion", "All things considered", "To sum up", "All in all", "It is not easy to reach any definite conclusions", "If any conclusions may be drawn from the data", "It is clear that", "The survey shows / indicates / demonstrates"

The Five Steps to Creating a Survey Report

  1. Create a questionnaire. Before creating, you must:
    • Define your objectives — your research objective guides the kind of questionnaire to create.
    • Identify your audience — the target audience determines the questions.
    • Choose a method — quantitative, qualitative, or both.
    • Choose question types — dichotomous, close-ended, rating, or ranking. Choose carefully so as not to affect the response rate (e.g., a phone-number question should not be closed-ended).
  2. Collect data.
  3. Analyse data.
  4. Analyse and interpret.
  5. Write the survey report.

Mental model: Design Questionnaire → Collect → Analyse → Interpret → Write.

Annotated Survey-Report Template

SURVEY REPORT TITLE (Specific, identifies subject and function) 1) INTRODUCTION - Purpose / aim of the report - Who commissioned it (e.g., "This report was commissioned by …") - When and how the information was gathered - Method: questionnaire / interviews / observation - Target audience / sample size 2) FINDINGS (MAIN BODY) - Present data clearly and in detail - Use subheadings / numbers / letters to separate items - Break down respondents into groups (age, sex, place, etc.) - State the main differences between groups - Use percentages, proportions, and approximate expressions e.g. "70% of respondents …", "one in four …", "the majority …", "a significant number …" - Use passive voice and reporting verbs e.g. "It was found that …", "The data indicates …" 3) CONCLUSION - Sum up the points mentioned above - State key insights - "If any conclusions may be drawn from the data, …" 4) RECOMMENDATIONS (if applicable) - Actionable steps based on the findings - Use modal verbs: "should", "ought to", "needs to" LANGUAGE CHECKLIST [ ] Formal, impersonal tone (no I / we / you) [ ] Present tenses + reported speech [ ] Passive constructions where appropriate [ ] Variety of reporting verbs [ ] Accurate, faithful representation of data

Sample Survey Report (from course material)

Title: Survey of Academic and General Reading in English

"On 8th February 1999, a survey was conducted among 16 overseas postgraduate students at the University of England. The purpose of the survey was to discover the reading habits in English of the students."

"The survey was conducted by means of a questionnaire given to the students to complete. The first part of the questionnaire dealt with the type of reading and its frequency. The second section was concerned with newspapers: the type of items read and those that were read first."

"From the table of data, the most significant items are as follows. In the first section 81% of the students regularly read academic books, while 44% regularly read academic journals. Nothing else is read regularly or often by 40% or more of the students. 75% sometimes read regional or local newspapers, 69% sometimes read books of fiction, 62% sometimes read general magazines, and 56% sometimes read national daily newspapers. On the other hand, 37% never read Sunday newspapers and 31% never read fiction."

"In the second section, not surprisingly, 100% read news about their own country in newspapers and 56% read this first. 94% read international news, 25% read this first. 81% read about Britain and look at radio and TV information. The only other item that is usually read by more than 50% of the students is current affairs (read by 56%)."

"If any conclusions may be drawn from the data, they are, perhaps, as follows. Overseas students presumably have little time for general reading: most of their reading time is spent on books and journals on their own subject. Outside their studies, apart from reading news about their own country, international news, and news about Britain, they probably spend most time watching TV and listening to the radio."

— from Academic Writing Course by R. R. Jordan

A second sample — internal report on employee benefits

An internal report has four typical sections, each using a different language strategy:

  • Terms of Reference — passive: "This report was commissioned by the personnel department…"
  • Findings — passive + percentages: "It was found that 70% of employees were satisfied with the overall package, and of these 25% were very satisfied… 10% expressed extreme dissatisfaction."
  • Conclusions — modal verbs: "it can be concluded that there is a need to look into the following areas… our benefits request system ought to be revised… improvements need to take place…"
  • Recommendations — active, first person, action verbs: "I recommend that we should take the following steps…"

Why this works: passive structures in sections 1–3 keep the report objective and impersonal; active structures in section 4 make the writer take ownership of the recommendations. Numbered points keep each finding/action trackable. Modal verbs in the conclusion (ought to, should) communicate strong recommendation while remaining diplomatic.

Three correct tips for writing internal reports

  1. Use clear, precise, and concise language.
  2. Use bulleted or numbered points.
  3. Make sure any conclusions or recommendations are based on facts.

Avoid: giving strong personal opinions throughout; using complex phrases and descriptive adjectives.

Common Mistakes

⚠️ Common Mistakes — Survey Reports
  • Using personal opinions (I think, we think, in my opinion) instead of an impersonal tone.
  • Dumping raw data instead of summarising responses with percentages and proportions.
  • Treating findings as insights — stating "60% reported slow loading" is a finding; explaining why is the insight.
  • Inaccurate data representation — survey reports must be faithful.
  • Writing the body as one long paragraph instead of using subheadings / numbered points.
  • Mixing complex phrases and descriptive adjectives instead of clear, concise language.
  • Recommendations that are not based on the facts presented in the findings.
  • Forgetting to break respondents into groups (age, sex, place) — losing the most important comparisons.

Topic 03 · Decision Report

Feasibility Report

A feasibility report does not describe a project — it evaluates it. It answers the questions every decision-maker asks: Can we do it? Should we do it? What will it cost? What are the risks?

Definition & Purpose

A feasibility report is a report that evaluates whether a project is:

  • Practical
  • Viable
  • Worth implementing

Why it matters:

  • Prevents failure
  • Saves time and cost
  • Identifies risks
  • Supports funding approval

Example scenario: "A university wants to build a new lab. Should it proceed or not?" — a feasibility report answers this by analysing whether the lab can be built, should be built, what it will cost, and what its risks and benefits are.

Core principle: A feasibility report is not describing a project. It is evaluating it. Every section exists to support a yes/no/conditional decision.

Feasibility Dimensions — The Decision Framework

A complete feasibility analysis evaluates the project across five dimensions. Each asks a different question.

1. Technical Feasibility

Can we build it?

  • Do we have the technology, tools, and skills?
  • Is the system compatible and scalable?
  • Tools + systems + challenges.
2. Economic Feasibility

Is it worth the cost?

  • Can it be funded?
  • Do the benefits outweigh the costs?
  • Development cost, maintenance, unexpected expenses, profitability — cost + revenue + benefits.
3. Operational Feasibility

Will it work in real life?

  • Can it function in real conditions?
  • Does it fit the organisation and user?
  • User acceptance, management support, business environment — staff + management + processes.
4. Legal Feasibility

Is it allowed?

  • Will people and regulations support it?
  • Does it comply with laws and regulations?
  • Data privacy, licensing, compliance — laws + compliance + risks.
5. Schedule Feasibility

Can we finish on time?

  • Can we complete it on time?
  • Are deadlines realistic?
  • Phase-based timeline.

Full Structure of a Feasibility Report

The course material orders the report as follows:

1) Executive Summary

A one-paragraph summary for the decision maker. It must include:

  • Project name
  • What is being proposed (context)
  • What problem it solves (purpose)
  • Brief feasibility outcome (analysis)
  • Overall conclusion

2) Problem

Purpose: explain why the system is needed.

  • Current issues
  • Limitations
  • Real-world issues
  • Current situation → Problem → Impact

3) Proposed Solution

Purpose: explain what you are building.

  • System idea
  • Key features
  • How it solves the problem
  • What technology & how it is used

4) Resource Requirements

Identifies what is needed to complete the project successfully — everything needed to build and run the project.

  • Material
  • Workforce
  • Technology systems
  • Furniture & operations

5) Decision Framework — the five feasibility dimensions

Apply each of the five dimensions in sequence — Technical → Economic → Operational → Legal → Schedule — and report the verdict for each (see grid above).

6) Conclusion and Recommendation

Purpose: the final decision.

  • Feasibility result — the bottom-line verdict (feasible / not feasible / feasible with conditions).
  • Recommendation — what should be done next.

How to write the Conclusion specifically: restate the project, give the overall feasibility outcome across all five dimensions, and end with a clear, action-oriented recommendation. Keep it brief, neutral, and focused on the decision-maker's question.

Key Language — Hedging, Conditional Phrasing, Objective Tone

A feasibility report uses a careful evaluative tone — it must be honest about uncertainty without being either dismissive or overly optimistic.

  • Hedging / cautious language — "the system appears to be technically feasible", "the project is likely to deliver…", "expected benefits include…"
  • Conditional phrasing — "the project will be viable provided that funding is secured", "if the schedule is extended by two months…", "subject to legal approval…"
  • Objective, evaluative tone — no marketing language, no personal opinion. Use evaluative phrases such as "the benefits outweigh the costs", "compliance risks are acceptable", "deadlines are realistic".

The course material explicitly emphasises an evaluating stance, balanced consideration of risks vs. benefits, and acknowledgement of constraints in cost, maintenance, and technical/environmental limitations.

Common Mistakes

⚠️ Common Mistakes — Feasibility Reports
  • Describing the project instead of evaluating it — the report must answer "should we do this?", not "what is this?".
  • Skipping one of the five feasibility dimensions (technical, economic, operational, legal, schedule).
  • Treating the report as a marketing pitch — feasibility is balanced and objective.
  • Ignoring risks and limitations — credibility requires honest constraints (cost, maintenance, technical/environmental).
  • Mixing the problem with the solution.
  • Recommendation that is not supported by the analysis.
  • Executive Summary that is too long — it must remain a single, decision-ready paragraph.
  • Schedules with unrealistic deadlines (no phase-based timeline).

Topic 04 · Status Report

Progress Report

A progress report (also called a status report) informs readers about a project that is not yet completed. Its main focus is to provide information and record project events for readers who are not involved in day-to-day operations.

Definition, Purpose & Audience

A progress report (also called a status report) informs readers about a project that is not yet completed.

  • The number of progress reports for any project usually is established at the outset, but more may be called for as the project continues.
  • They are often required in construction or research projects so that decision-makers can assess costs and the potential for successful completion by established deadlines.
  • Although a progress report may contain recommendations, its main focus is to provide information.
  • It records the project events for readers who are not involved in day-to-day operations.

Audience

Progress reports go to multiple readers with varying interests. The example in the course material is a dam-repair progress report addressed to one vice-president, with copies to four other managers — so the writer is actually preparing a report that will be used by at least five readers.

Series principle: The progress reports for a particular project make up a series, so keep your organisation consistent from report to report to aid readers who are following one particular aspect of the project.

Full Structure of a Progress Report

1) Introduction

The introduction reminds readers about progress to date. Explain the scope and purpose of the project, and identify it by specific title if there is one. Follow these guidelines:

  • State the precise dates covered by this particular report.
  • Define important technical terms for non-expert readers.
  • Identify the major stages of the project, if appropriate.
  • Summarise the previous progress achieved (after the first report in the series) so regular readers can recall the situation and new readers can become acquainted with the project.
  • Review any changes in the scope of the project since it began.

Example from the course material: The writer in Model 12-4 numbers her report and names the project in her subject line to help readers identify where this report fits in the series. In her introduction she establishes the dates covered, summarises previous repairs, and indicates that the project is close to schedule but that expected costs have risen because another subcontractor had to be hired.

2) Work Completed

This section describes the work completed since the preceding report and can be organised in two ways:

  • By tasks — describe the progress of each task chronologically.
  • By chronology — describe events according to a succession of dates and times.

Choose the organisation that best fits what your readers will find useful, and use subheadings to guide them to specific topics:

  • If your readers are interested primarily in certain segments of the project, task-oriented organisation is appropriate.
  • If your readers are interested only in the overall progress of the project, strict chronological organisation is probably better.

Follow these guidelines:

  • Describe the tasks that have been completed in the time covered by the report.
  • Give the dates relevant to each task.
  • Describe any equipment changes.
  • Explain special costs or personnel charges involved in the work completed.
  • Explain any problems or delays.
  • Explain why changes from the original plans were made.
  • Indicate whether the schedule dates were met.

3) Work Remaining

The section covering work remaining includes both the next immediate steps and those in the future. Place the most emphasis on the tasks that will be covered in your next progress report. Avoid overly optimistic promises.

Follow these guidelines:

  • Describe the major tasks that will be covered in the next report.
  • State the expected dates of completion for each task.
  • Mention briefly those tasks that are further in the future.

4) Adjustments / Problems

This section covers issues that have changed the original plan or time frame of the project since the last progress report. If the project is proceeding on schedule with no changes, this section is not needed.

If necessary, follow these guidelines:

  • Describe major obstacles that have arisen since the last progress report. (Do not discuss minor daily irritations.)
  • Explain needed changes in schedules.
  • Explain needed changes in the scope of the project or in specific tasks.
  • Explain problems in meeting original cost estimates.

5) Conclusion

The conclusion of a progress report summarises the status of the project and forecasts future progress. If your readers are not experts in the technical aspects of the project, they may rely heavily on the conclusion to provide them with an overall view of the project.

Follow these guidelines:

  • Report any progress on current stages.
  • Report any lack of progress on current stages.
  • Evaluate the overall progress so far.
  • Recommend any needed changes in minor areas of scheduling or planning.
  • State whether the project is worth continuing and is still expected to yield results.

Periodic vs. One-Time Progress Reports

Periodic (Series)
  • The number of reports is established at the outset.
  • They form a series — organisation must be consistent from report to report.
  • After the first report, the introduction must summarise previous progress.
  • Each subsequent report describes work completed since the preceding report.
  • Common in construction and long research projects.
One-Time
  • Submitted for a project that does not require ongoing tracking.
  • No need to recap previous reports — the introduction simply sets scope, purpose, and dates.
  • The Adjustments / Problems section may be omitted if the project is on schedule with no changes.
  • The conclusion is still required — overall progress and whether the project is worth continuing.

How to Write About Delays and Problems Diplomatically

The course material is explicit: avoid overly optimistic promises, do not discuss minor daily irritations, and the writer should identify major obstacles, explain needed schedule and scope changes, and explain problems in meeting cost estimates — factually and without drama.

Two excellent diplomatic moves from the model letters:

  • State the cause neutrally, then state the corrective action: "As a result of cracking of the foundation caused by the unexpected cold weather, Ace dug up the North side of the foundation and erected new forms on Friday, March 17, and will return to the site on Thursday, March 30, to repour the concrete."
  • Reassure on impact: "This change does not affect price or delivery time for the fixtures. The estimated delivery date is June 1."
  • Acknowledge a delay but commit to no further slippage: The engineer in Model 12-4 "assures her readers that, although she is three days behind schedule, she expects no further delays."

Tone: factual, forward-looking, professional

Across the entire report, the language is factual, specific with dates and figures, and forward-looking — every problem is paired with the corrective step and the next milestone. The writer's job is to keep readers informed and confident, not to entertain or apologise.

Topic 05 · Persuasion Report

Technical Proposal Report

A technical proposal explains what problem exists, why action is required now, and how a specific solution will be delivered. It must be persuasive but objective — and credible above all else.

Solicited vs. Unsolicited Proposals

Technical proposals are classified along three axes — who asked for it, who is it for, and why is it written. The most important pair for the exam is the initiation-based classification.

Solicited Proposal

Written in response to a formal Request for Proposal (RFP). The problem is already defined; your job is to respond with the best solution.

Example: A university invites companies to propose a Learning Management System.

Unsolicited Proposal

Written without any request. You identify the problem and convince the audience it is worth solving — requiring strong persuasion.

Example: A startup proposes solar charging stations to a city authority.

Other Classifications of Proposals

Internal Proposal (Audience)

Written within the same organisation. Focus is on improvement or operational efficiency. Tone is less formal; shared context is assumed. Example: An IT department proposes upgrading lab computers at a university.

External Proposal (Audience)

Written for outside clients or organisations. More formal and structured. Must establish credibility and clearly communicate benefits to an unfamiliar audience. Example: A software company proposes a cybersecurity system to a bank.

Planning Proposal (Purpose)

Focuses on how a project will be executed. Includes step-by-step methodology, timeline, and resource plan. Example: A CS team proposes a campus attendance management system — phases, timeline, tools, team roles.

Research Grant Proposal (Purpose)

Written to secure funding for academic or scientific research. Emphasises the research problem, methodology, and significance of expected outcomes. Example: Research on AI-based plagiarism detection, requesting funding.

Sales Proposal (Purpose)

Written to sell a product or service. Centres on client benefits, ROI, and persuasive value propositions rather than technical detail. Example: A tech company proposes a cloud-based cybersecurity system to a bank.

Full Structure of a Technical Proposal

The course material lays out the following sections in order:

1) Background

Purpose: set the scene before introducing the issue. Begin broadly, then narrow your focus to the relevant context. Use a neutral, factual tone.

  • General situation
  • Current practices / conditions
  • Relevant trends (technology use, demand, etc.)

Common mistakes in this section:

  • Starting directly with the problem.
  • Giving opinions instead of context.
  • Writing extensive detail too soon in the proposal.

2) Statement of the Problem — Identify the Gap

Purpose: clearly identify the specific gap, who is affected, and the consequence of inaction.

  • Be specific — name the exact issue, not a general complaint.
  • Show impact — who is affected and what the consequences are.
  • Show consequences.
  • Stay focused — do not mix the problem with proposed solutions.

Common mistakes in this section:

  • Writing vague problems.
  • Repeating the background instead of identifying a gap.
  • Mixing problem with solution.

Rule: be precise and direct, avoid vague language.

3) Need — Why Action Is Required

Purpose: justify why the problem must be solved now. Build logically on the problem statement to convey urgency.

  • Necessity — link directly to measurable improvement.
  • Logical reasoning — build on the stated problem to justify a solution.
  • Urgency — explain how the problem can worsen without intervention.

Common mistakes:

  • Repeating the problem.
  • Too general.
  • Not showing urgency.

Rule: avoid repeating the content of the problem section.

4) Benefits — Value of the Solution

Demonstrate concrete value. Focus on outcomes, not features. You may list two to three specific, outcome-oriented advantages:

  • Practical
  • Technical
  • Social / environmental

5) Limitations — Show Realism

Acknowledging limitations builds credibility. Reviewers trust proposals that are honest about constraints. Keep it balanced: neither dismissive nor overly negative.

  • Cost and budget constraints
  • Maintenance and operational challenges
  • Technical or environmental limitations

6) Scope — Define the Boundaries

Purpose: define the precise boundaries of your proposal — what is covered and what is not covered.

Within Scope (examples)
  • Target area, location, or user group
  • Specified systems or processes addressed
  • Defined deliverables and coverage
Out of Scope (examples)
  • Areas or systems not addressed
  • Future phases or extensions
  • Tasks or coverage outside the project

7) Proposed Plan — Implementation

Purpose: explain how the solution will be carried out in practice.

What to include:

  • What will be done.
  • How it will be implemented.
  • Basic steps or phases.

How to Write a Persuasive Yet Professional Proposal

The course material defines the proposal as a persuasive document — especially in the unsolicited and sales varieties — but consistently demands a neutral, factual tone in the background, a direct and precise problem statement, and logical, measurable justification in the need section.

The persuasive engine of a proposal sits in three places:

  • Need — urgency and consequences of inaction.
  • Benefits — outcome-oriented advantages (practical, technical, social/environmental).
  • Limitations — honesty that increases credibility.

The role of evidence, feasibility, and credibility

  • Evidence — every claim in the problem and need sections must be backed by specific impact (who is affected, what consequences). Vague claims undermine credibility.
  • Feasibility — a strong proposal demonstrates that the proposed plan is realistic; the implementation section translates the solution into basic steps or phases that the reader can evaluate.
  • Credibility — built by honest acknowledgement of limitations, clear scope boundaries, and the neutral, factual tone demanded by the background section.

Action-oriented language — use clear action verbs in the plan section: analyse, design, develop, evaluate, implement.

Common Mistakes

⚠️ Common Mistakes — Technical Proposal
  • Background: starting directly with the problem; giving opinions instead of context; writing extensive detail too soon.
  • Problem: writing vague problems; repeating background instead of identifying a gap; mixing problem with solution.
  • Need: repeating the problem; being too general; not showing urgency.
  • Benefits: listing features instead of outcomes.
  • Limitations: hiding them — reviewers trust proposals that are honest about constraints.
  • Scope: failing to state what is out of scope — leaves the proposal open-ended.

Topic 06 · Workplace Writing

Writing Professional Emails

Every professional email answers one question for the recipient: "Why should I open this and what do you need from me?" The course material treats the email as a structured request, with specific moves for the subject line, body, follow-up, and recipient address.

General Structure of an Email

Subject Line

Concise description of your email's purpose. Ask: "Why should I, as a recipient, open this email?"

Purpose

Why are you sending this email — phrased as a request.

Impact (Optional)

What impact can their response have on you? Optional, but most useful when the request does not have a clear or immediate impact.

Action

What information do you need in their response?

Context (Optional)

Information to help them understand your situation and complete your request. Optional and should be kept minimal if added — include only what is necessary.

Mental model: Subject → Purpose → Impact → Action → Context. The Impact and Context steps are conditional — add them only when they help.

Rules for Writing Effective Subject Lines

A subject line is a concise description of your email's purpose. Often emails are time-sensitive or have a simple but important request (e.g., a signature on a document).

Consider including this information in brackets [LIKE SO] followed by your subject line — for example:

  • [URGENT] Signature required on enrolment form by Friday
  • [ACTION REQUIRED] Approval needed for Project Plan v2
  • [FYI] Updated meeting notes attached

This makes time-sensitivity or the specific request visible before the reader even opens the email.

Tone Levels — Formal vs. Semi-Formal

The course material treats formality as a spectrum chosen by the audience. The default position is summarised by the principle below.

Default rule: "When you are not sure how the recipient likes to be addressed, always choose the side of too formal." Use formal salutations and titles when in doubt. Semi-formal tone is reserved for established relationships and internal/peer communication where shared context is already assumed.

Addressing Your Recipient

Some titles you may encounter, in decreasing order of formality:

  1. Professor (Prof.)
  2. Doctor (Dr.) — used to refer to any individual with a PhD or equivalent doctorate degree (e.g., postdoctoral researcher).
  3. Mr. / Ms. (avoid Mrs. or Miss)

When you don't know who the recipient is

Make the best guess of their title.

  • Emailing for job applications — "Dear hiring manager" (if the posting does not contain a contact name).
  • Emailing a fellowship organisation — "Dear reviewing committee of XY fellowship".
  • General unknown recipient — "To whom it may concern".

Follow-Up Emails

People are incredibly busy, so emails often don't get a response. This could be due to the email being missed, forgotten, or your request being unclear. Depending on the importance and time-sensitivity, you may want to send a follow-up.

  • A follow-up email serves as a gentle reminder of your original email.
  • A follow-up email should be short and polite, reminding your recipient of your core request.

When to send a follow-up?

  • Consider typical working hours — professors / professionals may not read emails received outside of working hours (9–5, Monday–Friday), on weekends, or on holidays.
  • An email sent Friday night may not receive a response until the following Wednesday.
  • For non-urgent emails: wait 5–10 business days before sending a follow-up — that is the standard.
  • For urgent emails: waiting 2–3 business days may be more appropriate.

Email Timing Etiquette

  • Many people only read and respond to emails during normal working hours, 9 AM – 5 PM Monday through Friday.
  • Unless you have an established relationship and know they will read your email outside those hours, it is most respectful to send emails during that time period.
  • Non-urgent emails generally should not be sent outside of normal working hours, regardless of your relationship to the recipient.
  • When working with people in other time zones, try to send emails during the working hours of their time zone.

Text Formatting

Using bolding, underlines, and/or italics can be useful to emphasise key information within your email.

  • Use them sparingly — if everything is bolded, nothing will be emphasised.
  • Avoid other formatting such as different fonts, text colours, etc.

Other text formatting options — indents, bullet points, and line breaks — can also be used to create a more readable email.

  • Numbered bullet points are particularly useful for emails which contain multiple requests.
  • This allows your reader to keep track of each individual request and provides them a template to respond with.

Confirmation of Email Receipt

Some emails may not have a specific request that requires a response — for example, sending a professor a document they requested.

To verify your recipient has seen your message, you can request a confirmation of receipt in your email:

"Please confirm when you have received this document."

CC, BCC & Reply-All Etiquette

The course material's primary worked example is a progress report addressed to one vice-president with copies to four other managers with varying interests in the project. The writer therefore knows she is actually preparing a report that will be used by at least five readers.

The principle that follows from this — and from the email module's emphasis on respecting the recipient's time, the recipient's working hours, and on keeping the request clear — is:

  • CC: use only when the additional readers genuinely have an interest in the topic, and remember that you are writing for all of them, not only the primary recipient.
  • BCC: reserve for cases where a third party needs visibility but the primary recipient does not need to know.
  • Reply-All: only when every original recipient still needs the new information. Otherwise reply only to the sender — respect everyone else's working hours and inbox.
