1 Instructional Writing

Definition

Instructional writing is a form of technical communication that provides step-by-step directions to help a reader complete a specific task. Its primary aim is clarity: the reader should be able to follow the instructions and achieve the desired outcome without confusion or ambiguity.

Purpose

  • Guide users through processes, procedures, or tasks in a logical sequence.
  • Reduce errors, safety hazards, and the need for additional support.
  • Serve as a permanent, reusable reference (manuals, SOPs, how-to guides).
  • Ensure consistency when multiple people perform the same task.
Key Principle
Instructional writing is reader-centred. Every decision — word choice, step order, visual aids — should be made with the target audience in mind.

Key Components of Instructional Writing

  1. Title: A concise, descriptive heading that tells the reader exactly what the instructions will help them accomplish (e.g., "How to Set Up a Wireless Router").
  2. Introduction / Overview: A brief statement of purpose, the intended audience, and any prerequisites (materials, tools, prior knowledge).
  3. Materials / Requirements List: Everything the reader needs before beginning, presented in a bulleted list.
  4. Sequential Steps: Numbered, action-oriented sentences, each beginning with a command verb (e.g., "Click," "Insert," "Attach").
  5. Warnings / Cautions / Notes: Safety alerts or important information placed before the step they relate to, visually distinct from regular text.
  6. Visuals: Diagrams, screenshots, or illustrations that supplement written steps.
  7. Expected Outcome / Conclusion: A brief description of what the reader should see or have achieved upon completion.
  8. Troubleshooting (optional): Common problems and their solutions.

Step-by-step Guide: How to Write Effective Instructions

  1. Identify your audience. Determine their knowledge level (beginner, intermediate, expert). This controls vocabulary, detail level, and the inclusion of background information.
  2. Define the task scope. Clearly decide where the process starts and ends. Avoid scope creep — one set of instructions per task.
  3. Gather information. Perform the task yourself, interview subject-matter experts, or consult existing documentation.
  4. List all materials and prerequisites. Place these before the first step so the reader can prepare everything in advance.
  5. Write steps in chronological order. Each step should describe exactly one action. Use imperative mood ("Press the button," not "You should press the button").
  6. Add warnings before the relevant step. Never bury safety information inside or after a step — the reader may act before reading ahead.
  7. Use consistent formatting. Number each step. Use bold for UI elements or key objects. Use bullet lists for sub-steps.
  8. Include visuals where helpful. A labelled diagram or screenshot can replace paragraphs of description.
  9. State the expected result. End with what the reader should observe ("The LED will turn solid green").
  10. Test your instructions. Have someone from the target audience follow your instructions. Revise based on their feedback.

Examples

Example 1 — How to Create a Strong Password

Good Example
HOW TO CREATE A STRONG PASSWORD

Purpose: This guide helps you create a secure password
that protects your online accounts from unauthorized access.

Requirements:
• Access to the account or service where you want to set a password.
• A password manager application (recommended).

Steps:
1. Open the account settings or registration page of the website.
2. Navigate to the "Password" or "Security" section.
3. Choose a password that is at least 12 characters long.
4. Include at least:
   – One uppercase letter (A–Z)
   – One lowercase letter (a–z)
   – One number (0–9)
   – One special character (!, @, #, $, etc.)
5. Avoid using personal information such as your name,
   birthday, or common words like "password" or "123456."
6. Type the password into the "New Password" field.
7. Re-type it in the "Confirm Password" field.
8. Click "Save" or "Update Password."

⚠ WARNING: Never share your password with anyone or
write it on a sticky note attached to your monitor.

Expected Result:
Your password is now updated. You will use this new
password the next time you log in.

Example 2 — How to Send a Professional Email

Good Example
HOW TO SEND A PROFESSIONAL EMAIL

Purpose: This guide walks you through composing and
sending a clear, professional email.

Requirements:
• An active email account (e.g., Gmail, Outlook).
• The recipient's email address.

Steps:
1. Open your email application and click "Compose"
   or "New Email."
2. In the "To" field, enter the recipient's email address.
   Double-check for typos.
3. Write a concise, specific subject line.
   – Good: "Meeting Rescheduled to March 15"
   – Bad: "Hi" or "(no subject)"
4. Start with a professional greeting:
   – "Dear Professor Khan," (formal)
   – "Hi Sarah," (semi-formal, if appropriate)
5. State the purpose of your email in the first sentence.
6. Provide necessary details in the body. Use short
   paragraphs and bullet points for readability.
7. End with a polite closing:
   – "Best regards," / "Sincerely," / "Thank you,"
8. Add your full name and any relevant contact
   information below the closing.
9. Proofread the entire email for spelling, grammar,
   and tone.
10. Click "Send."

Expected Result:
The recipient receives a clear, courteous, and
professional email.

Contrast: Bad Instructional Writing

Bad Example
SENDING AN EMAIL

So basically you just open your email and type stuff in.
Make sure you put the address in the right spot and write
something in the subject. Then just write your message and
hit send. It's pretty easy honestly.

Why is it bad? No numbered steps, vague language ("type stuff"), no audience awareness, missing prerequisites, no expected result, and an unprofessional tone.

Do's & Don'ts

Do

  • Use numbered steps for sequential tasks.
  • Begin each step with an action verb.
  • Keep one action per step.
  • Place warnings before the step.
  • Write for your specific audience.
  • Test instructions with real users.
  • Use visuals to complement text.

Don't

  • Use jargon without defining it (for beginners).
  • Combine multiple actions in one step.
  • Assume the reader "just knows" things.
  • Use passive voice ("The button should be clicked").
  • Skip the materials/prerequisites list.
  • Write in paragraph form instead of steps.
  • Place warnings after the dangerous step.
Common Exam Mistakes
  • Students write instructions in paragraph form instead of numbered steps.
  • Steps lack command verbs — e.g., "The file" instead of "Open the file."
  • Missing title, audience statement, or expected outcome.
  • Warnings placed after the step where the hazard occurs.
Exam Tips
  • Always include a title, introduction, materials, numbered steps, and an expected result — examiners look for structure.
  • Use the imperative mood consistently. It immediately signals instructional writing.
  • If asked to critique bad instructions, identify: vague language, missing steps, no audience awareness, and poor formatting.

2 Resume & Cover Letter

Resume — Definition & Purpose

A resume (also curriculum vitae in some regions) is a concise, structured document that summarizes your education, skills, experience, and achievements. Its purpose is to persuade a potential employer to invite you for an interview.

Key Fact
Recruiters spend an average of 6–7 seconds on an initial resume scan. Clarity, formatting, and relevance are critical.

Resume Structure (Sections)

SectionContent
Header / Contact InformationFull name, phone number, professional email, LinkedIn URL (optional), city & country.
Objective / Summary2–3 sentences stating your career goal and key qualifications. Tailor to each job.
EducationDegree(s), institution name, graduation date, GPA (if strong), honours/awards.
Work ExperienceJob title, company, dates; bullet-point achievements (not just duties). Use action verbs and quantify results.
SkillsTechnical skills, software, languages, certifications relevant to the position.
Projects (optional)Academic or personal projects demonstrating practical abilities.
Extracurricular / Volunteer (optional)Leadership roles, community service, relevant activities.
References"Available upon request" or list 2–3 professional references with contact details.

Formatting Best Practices

  • Keep it to one page (for students and early-career professionals).
  • Use a clean, professional font (e.g., Calibri, Garamond, Arial) at 10–12 pt.
  • Maintain consistent margins (0.5"–1").
  • Use reverse chronological order (most recent first) for education and experience.
  • Use bullet points, not paragraphs, under each role.
  • Start bullets with action verbs: Developed, Managed, Designed, Increased, Led.
  • Quantify achievements: "Increased sales by 20%" rather than "Helped with sales."
  • No photos, fancy graphics, or coloured backgrounds (unless design field).

Cover Letter — Definition & Purpose

A cover letter is a one-page document that accompanies your resume. It introduces you to the employer, explains why you are interested in the position, and highlights the most relevant qualifications that make you a strong candidate.

Cover Letter Structure

PartContent
HeaderYour contact information, date, employer's contact information.
Salutation"Dear Mr./Ms. [Last Name]," or "Dear Hiring Manager," if the name is unknown.
Opening ParagraphState the position you are applying for, where you found it, and a compelling hook (why you are excited about this role).
Body Paragraph(s)Connect your skills and experiences to the job requirements. Use specific examples. Explain what you can contribute.
Closing ParagraphRestate your enthusiasm, mention enclosed resume, request an interview, and provide contact details.
Sign-off"Sincerely," / "Best regards," followed by your full name.

Do's & Don'ts — Resume & Cover Letter

Do

  • Tailor each resume and cover letter to the specific job.
  • Use action verbs and quantify achievements.
  • Keep resume to one page (students).
  • Address the cover letter to a specific person if possible.
  • Proofread multiple times.
  • Use a professional email address.
  • Match the visual style of the resume and cover letter.

Don't

  • Use a generic objective for every application.
  • List duties instead of achievements.
  • Include personal details like age, religion, or marital status.
  • Use "To Whom It May Concern."
  • Repeat the resume word-for-word in the cover letter.
  • Use unprofessional email (coolboy99@...).
  • Include irrelevant hobbies ("I enjoy sleeping").
Common Exam Mistakes
  • Writing a cover letter that simply restates the resume without adding context or personality.
  • Forgetting to include quantified achievements ("Managed social media" vs. "Grew Instagram following by 40% in 3 months").
  • Incorrect formatting: inconsistent fonts, missing dates, no header.
  • Objective statement too vague: "I want a good job" — gives the examiner nothing to evaluate.
Exam Tips
  • If asked to "write a resume," include all sections (header, objective, education, experience, skills, references) even if some are fictitious.
  • For cover letters, clearly show the three-paragraph structure: opening hook → body evidence → closing call-to-action.
  • If given a "bad resume" to improve, check for: missing action verbs, no quantification, poor formatting, irrelevant content, and grammatical errors.

3 How to Read a Research Paper

Definition & Purpose

Reading a research paper is a structured analytical skill — not casual reading. The goal is to extract the paper's contribution, evaluate its methodology, understand its findings, and determine its relevance to your own work or study.

Why This Matters
In academic and professional settings, you will regularly read papers for literature reviews, project research, and staying current in your field. Efficient reading saves time and improves comprehension.

Sections of a Research Paper

SectionPurposeWhat to Look For
Title & Abstract Summarize the entire paper in ~150–300 words. Research question, method, key finding, conclusion. Decide if the paper is relevant.
Introduction Provide background, state the problem, and present the research objective. Motivation for the study, gap in existing knowledge, thesis / research question.
Literature Review Survey existing work related to the topic. Key references, how this work differs from or builds upon prior studies.
Methodology Describe how the research was conducted. Research design, data collection, tools, sample size, variables, reproducibility.
Results Present findings without interpretation. Tables, graphs, statistical data. Look for patterns and anomalies.
Discussion Interpret results and relate them to the research question. Authors' explanation of findings, limitations, comparison with other studies.
Conclusion Summarize contributions and suggest future work. Main takeaway, practical implications, areas for further research.
References List all cited sources. Useful for finding related work and verifying claims.

Step-by-step Method: The Three-Pass Approach

Pass 1 — Skimming (5–10 minutes)

Goal: Decide if the paper is worth reading in depth.

  1. Read the title and abstract carefully.
  2. Read all section headings and sub-headings to understand the structure.
  3. Glance at figures, tables, and graphs — they often contain the core results.
  4. Read the conclusion to understand the paper's main claims.
  5. Scan the references — do you recognize any? This tells you the research community.
After Pass 1, answer
  • What type of paper is it? (empirical, theoretical, review, case study)
  • Is it relevant to my needs?
  • Do the claims seem reasonable?

Pass 2 — Comprehensive Reading (30–60 minutes)

Goal: Understand the content without worrying about every detail.

  1. Read the entire paper, but skip complex proofs or mathematical derivations on the first pass.
  2. Annotate as you go: highlight key claims, underline definitions, mark sections you don't understand.
  3. Study all figures and tables closely — read captions and axes labels.
  4. Identify the main arguments and the evidence supporting each.
  5. Note any terms or references you need to look up later.

Pass 3 — Critical Analysis (1–2 hours, for important papers)

Goal: Deeply understand and evaluate the paper.

  1. Re-read the methodology in detail. Could you reproduce this study?
  2. Evaluate the evidence: Is the sample size adequate? Are the statistical tests appropriate? Are there confounding variables?
  3. Challenge every assumption. What if the authors are wrong? What alternative explanations exist?
  4. Compare with other papers you've read on the same topic.
  5. Summarize the paper in your own words: problem, method, key findings, limitations, and your assessment.

Practical Example: Analyzing a Paper

Suppose you are reading: "The Impact of Social Media on Academic Performance Among University Students"

Analysis Walkthrough

Pass 1 — Skim:

  • Title: Clearly about social media + academic performance.
  • Abstract: Says the study surveyed 350 students and found a negative correlation between daily social media use (>3 hrs) and GPA.
  • Headings: Introduction, Literature Review, Methodology, Results, Discussion, Conclusion — standard structure.
  • Conclusion: Recommends universities implement digital wellness programs.
  • Decision: Relevant to my topic — proceed to Pass 2.

Pass 2 — Read:

  • Introduction: Motivation is the growing use of social media among students; problem is conflicting findings in prior studies.
  • Methodology: Quantitative survey, 350 respondents, Likert scale, correlation analysis.
  • Results: Table shows r = −0.42 (moderate negative correlation). Figure 3 plots GPA vs. hours on social media.
  • Discussion: Authors note that correlation ≠ causation; acknowledge self-reporting bias.

Pass 3 — Critique:

  • Strength: Decent sample size (350), standardized tool.
  • Weakness: Self-reported data, single university only (limits generalizability), no control for other variables (sleep, part-time work).
  • My assessment: Useful as supporting evidence but not conclusive on its own. Should be cited alongside studies with experimental or longitudinal designs.
Exam Tips
  • If asked "How do you read a research paper?", always mention the three-pass approach.
  • Be ready to explain what you look for in each section of a paper.
  • Practice identifying strengths and weaknesses — examiners often ask for critical evaluation.
  • Know the difference between correlation and causation — a frequent exam concept.

Do

  • Read the abstract and conclusion first.
  • Take notes and annotate actively.
  • Look up unfamiliar terms.
  • Evaluate methodology critically.
  • Summarize in your own words.

Don't

  • Read linearly like a novel.
  • Accept all claims at face value.
  • Ignore figures and tables.
  • Skip the references section.
  • Give up if the first pass is confusing — that's normal.

4 Introduction Writing

Overview

The introduction is the opening section of any research paper, report, or proposal. It sets the stage by telling the reader why the topic matters, what problem exists, and what the study aims to achieve. A well-written introduction contains three core elements:

  1. Motivation — Why should the reader care?
  2. Problem Statement — What specific gap or issue does this work address?
  3. Purpose Statement — What does this study intend to do about it?
The Funnel Structure
Introductions typically follow a funnel (general → specific) pattern: start with broad context (motivation), narrow down to the specific problem, and then state your purpose/objective.

Motivation

Definition

The motivation establishes the broader context of the research. It answers: "Why is this topic important?" and "Why should anyone care about this research?" It provides the background that justifies the existence of the study.

Purpose

  • Hook the reader's attention.
  • Establish the significance and relevance of the topic.
  • Provide necessary background information.
  • Create a logical bridge to the problem statement.

How to Write a Strong Motivation

  1. Start with the big picture. Introduce the general field or topic area.
  2. Cite relevant statistics or trends that show the topic's importance (e.g., "Over 4.9 billion people worldwide use social media as of 2025").
  3. Highlight real-world impact. Explain why this topic matters to society, industry, or academia.
  4. Connect to the reader's context. If your audience is students, relate the topic to their experience.
  5. Transition smoothly to the problem by indicating that despite progress, a gap or challenge remains.

Examples

Good Motivation

"The rapid growth of e-commerce has transformed consumer behaviour worldwide. In Pakistan alone, online retail revenue exceeded $6 billion in 2025, representing a 28% increase from the previous year (Pakistan Bureau of Statistics, 2025). As businesses increasingly shift to digital platforms, the need for effective cybersecurity measures has become paramount. Data breaches cost companies an average of $4.45 million per incident globally (IBM, 2024), making this not only a technical concern but a critical business challenge. However, despite this growing threat, small and medium enterprises (SMEs) continue to lag in adopting robust security frameworks."

Bad Motivation

"Cybersecurity is very important these days. Everyone knows that hacking is bad and we should protect our data. This paper is about cybersecurity."

Why is the bad version weak?

  • Too vague — no statistics, no specifics, no evidence.
  • Uses informal language ("Everyone knows").
  • Does not establish significance or context.
  • Jumps directly to the topic without building a logical argument.

Problem Statement

Definition

A problem statement is a clear, concise description of the specific issue, gap, or challenge that the research aims to address. It answers: "What is the specific problem this study focuses on?"

Purpose

  • Narrow the focus from the broad motivation to a specific, researchable problem.
  • Identify what is missing, broken, unknown, or inadequate in the current state of knowledge.
  • Justify the need for the study.
  • Give the reader a clear understanding of the research gap.

Characteristics of a Strong Problem Statement

  • Specific: Addresses a well-defined issue, not a broad topic.
  • Evidence-based: Supported by data, citations, or logical reasoning.
  • Significant: The problem is worth solving — it has real-world or academic impact.
  • Feasible: The problem can realistically be investigated within the scope of the study.
  • Gap-oriented: Clearly states what existing research has not addressed.

How to Write a Problem Statement

  1. Describe the ideal situation (what should be happening).
  2. Describe the reality (what is actually happening — supported by evidence).
  3. Identify the gap between the ideal and reality.
  4. State the consequences of this gap (why it matters if the problem is not solved).
  5. Formulate the problem as a concise statement or question.

Examples

Good Problem Statement

"While large corporations have invested significantly in cybersecurity infrastructure, small and medium enterprises (SMEs) in Pakistan remain highly vulnerable to cyber threats. A 2024 report by the Pakistan Telecommunication Authority found that 67% of SMEs lack a formal cybersecurity policy, and 43% experienced at least one data breach in the past year. Despite the availability of cost-effective security tools, there is a lack of research examining why SMEs fail to adopt these measures and what barriers — financial, educational, or organizational — prevent implementation."

Bad Problem Statement

"Small businesses have problems with cybersecurity. They get hacked sometimes and this is a big issue. Not much research has been done on this topic."

Why is the bad version weak?

  • No specifics — what kind of businesses? Where? How often?
  • Claim "not much research" is unsupported — no citation.
  • Doesn't identify a clear gap in the literature.
  • No data or evidence to support the claims.

Purpose Statement

Definition

A purpose statement declares the specific aim or objective of the research. It answers: "What does this study intend to accomplish?" It directly follows from the problem statement and states how the researcher plans to address the identified gap.

Purpose

  • Clearly communicate the research objective to the reader.
  • Define the scope and direction of the study.
  • Serve as a roadmap for the rest of the paper.

Key Elements

  • Action verb: "investigate," "examine," "analyze," "evaluate," "explore," "determine."
  • Focus: The specific phenomenon, variable, or population being studied.
  • Context: Where and when the study takes place.

Common Templates

"The purpose of this study is to [action verb] the [topic/variable]
among [population] in [context/location]."

"This research aims to [action verb] the [relationship/effect/impact]
of [Variable A] on [Variable B] in [setting]."

"The objective of this paper is to [action verb] the factors that
[influence/contribute to] [phenomenon] in [context]."

Examples

Good Purpose Statement

"The purpose of this study is to investigate the barriers preventing small and medium enterprises (SMEs) in Islamabad from adopting cybersecurity frameworks, with a specific focus on financial constraints, technical knowledge gaps, and organizational culture."

Another Good Example

"This research aims to evaluate the effectiveness of gamification techniques in improving student engagement in undergraduate programming courses at FAST-NUCES."

Bad Purpose Statement

"This paper talks about cybersecurity problems in small businesses."

Why is the bad version weak?

  • "Talks about" is vague — not an action verb showing clear intent.
  • No specific population, location, or variables.
  • Does not state what the study will actually do or investigate.

Complete Introduction Flow — Putting It All Together

Complete Introduction Example

[Motivation] The global education sector has undergone a dramatic transformation with the integration of technology into classrooms. Online learning platforms like Coursera, edX, and Khan Academy have made quality education accessible to millions. In Pakistan, the Higher Education Commission reported a 65% increase in online course enrolments between 2022 and 2025, highlighting the growing demand for digital education.

[Problem Statement] However, despite this surge in adoption, student completion rates for online courses remain alarmingly low — averaging just 12–15% globally (Jordan, 2024). Research has identified factors such as lack of motivation, poor course design, and insufficient instructor interaction as potential causes, yet few studies have focused on the specific challenges faced by Pakistani university students in completing self-paced online courses.

[Purpose Statement] The purpose of this study is to identify and analyse the factors contributing to low online course completion rates among undergraduate students at Pakistani universities, with a focus on motivation, course design, and peer interaction.

Common Exam Mistakes
  • Confusing motivation with problem statement — motivation is the "why it matters," not the "what's wrong."
  • Writing a purpose statement without an action verb (investigate, examine, analyse).
  • Making the problem statement too broad: "Education has problems" is not a problem statement.
  • Forgetting to connect the three elements — they should flow logically from one to the next.
Exam Tips
  • If asked to "write an introduction," always include all three parts: motivation → problem → purpose.
  • Label each part clearly if the question asks you to identify them.
  • Use the funnel structure: broad → narrow → specific.
  • Include at least one statistic or citation in your motivation and problem statement, even if fictional for exam purposes — it shows you understand the convention.
  • Memorize 2–3 strong action verbs for purpose statements: investigate, evaluate, examine, analyse, explore.

5 Exam Practice Questions

A. Conceptual Questions

  1. Define instructional writing. What are its key characteristics?
  2. List and explain the essential components of a well-structured set of instructions.
  3. What is the difference between a resume and a cover letter? How do they complement each other?
  4. Explain the three-pass approach to reading a research paper. What is the goal of each pass?
  5. Define motivation, problem statement, and purpose statement in the context of introduction writing. How are they different?
  6. Why should instructional writing use imperative mood? Give an example.
  7. What is the funnel structure in introduction writing? Illustrate with a brief outline.
  8. Why is it important to place warnings before the relevant step in instructional writing?
  9. What are the key sections of a research paper? Briefly describe the purpose of each.
  10. What makes a problem statement "strong"? List at least four characteristics.

B. Short-Answer Questions

  1. Rewrite the following in imperative mood: "The user should click the submit button after filling in all the fields."
  2. Identify three problems with this objective statement: "I want to get a good job at a nice company where I can learn stuff."
  3. List five action verbs suitable for resume bullet points.
  4. What does it mean to quantify achievements on a resume? Give two examples.
  5. Explain the difference between correlation and causation when evaluating research paper results.
  6. Write a one-sentence purpose statement for a study on the effects of sleep deprivation on exam performance among university students.
  7. What is a research gap? Why is it important in a problem statement?
  8. Name three things you should check during Pass 1 (skimming) of a research paper.

  • What questions would you ask during Pass 2 (comprehensive reading)?
